# Khilafat Movement (1919–1924), Non-Cooperation Movement (1920-22) & Swaraj Party (1923-26)

The Khilafat Movement (1919–1924) and the Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922) were closely connected mass movements against British rule. Gandhi supported the Khilafat cause and linked it with the nationalist struggle, helping to create a broad platform for Hindu–Muslim political cooperation.

The following notes cover their background, leaders, organisations, resolutions, programmes, important events, withdrawal, consequences and distinctions relevant to UPSC Prelims.

The notes distinguish the formal Non-Cooperation programme from related peasant, tribal and local movements, which did not always follow Congress instructions.

## 1. Historical Background

### 1.1 Factors leading to mass agitation

| Factor                                   | Significance                                                                           |
| ---------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| First World War (1914–1918)              | Economic hardship, inflation, taxation and political expectations                      |
| Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms, 1919         | Considered inadequate by many nationalists                                             |
| Rowlatt Act, 1919                        | Permitted extraordinary repressive measures                                            |
| Jallianwala Bagh massacre, 13 April 1919 | Intensified anger against British rule                                                 |
| Punjab repression                        | Deepened dissatisfaction with colonial administration                                  |
| Khilafat issue                           | Mobilised sections of Indian Muslims against British policy towards the Ottoman Empire |

Gandhi brought together three important grievances: the Punjab wrongs, the Khilafat wrongs and the demand for Swaraj.

## 2. Khilafat Movement (1919–1924)

### 2.1 Meaning and background

- The Ottoman Sultan also claimed the title of Caliph (Khalifa), regarded by many Sunni Muslims as a religious leader.
- The Ottoman Empire fought on the side of the Central Powers during the First World War and was defeated.
- After the war, the victorious Allied Powers proposed territorial and political restrictions on the Ottoman Empire.
- Many Indian Muslims feared that the Ottoman Sultan-Caliph's authority and the Ottoman Empire's territories would be undermined.
- The Khilafat Movement developed to pressure the British government to protect the position of the Caliph and secure favourable treatment for the Ottoman Empire.

Important distinction: The Khilafat Movement originated from concern about the Ottoman Caliphate, whereas the Non-Cooperation Movement was a broader Indian anti-colonial struggle.

### 2.2 Important leaders

| Leader                  | Association                                                  |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Maulana Muhammad Ali    | Prominent Khilafat leader; one of the Ali brothers           |
| Maulana Shaukat Ali     | Prominent Khilafat leader; brother of Muhammad Ali           |
| Maulana Abul Kalam Azad | Supported the Khilafat and nationalist movements             |
| Hakim Ajmal Khan        | Important leader associated with the Khilafat cause          |
| Hasrat Mohani           | Khilafat leader and advocate of complete independence        |
| Mahatma Gandhi          | Supported Khilafat and advocated non-violent non-cooperation |

Ali brothers: Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali.

### 2.3 Khilafat organisations and meetings

- The All-India Khilafat Committee emerged in Bombay in 1919 to coordinate agitation.
- Khilafat conferences and committees mobilised public opinion, organised meetings and pressed the British government to address Muslim concerns.
- Gandhi supported the Khilafat agitation as an opportunity to unite different communities in a common anti-colonial struggle.
- At the All-India Khilafat Conference in Delhi in November 1919, Gandhi advocated non-cooperation as a possible method of protest if Khilafat demands were not met.

### 2.4 Treaty of Sèvres (1920)

- The Treaty of Sèvres, signed on 10 August 1920, imposed severe terms on the defeated Ottoman Empire.
- It proposed extensive territorial losses and restrictions on Ottoman sovereignty.
- Its terms intensified Khilafat opposition in India.
- The treaty was subsequently superseded by the Treaty of Lausanne (1923).

### 2.5 Developments in Turkey and end of the Khilafat issue

| Year | Development                                                     |
| ---- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1920 | Treaty of Sèvres                                                |
| 1922 | Turkish Grand National Assembly abolished the Ottoman Sultanate |
| 1923 | Treaty of Lausanne; Republic of Turkey proclaimed               |
| 1924 | Turkish Grand National Assembly abolished the Caliphate         |

- Mustafa Kemal Atatürk led the Turkish nationalist movement.
- The Sultanate was abolished in 1922, but the Caliphate continued temporarily as a separate institution.
- The Caliphate was abolished on 3 March 1924.

Prelims distinction: Abolition of the Sultanate in 1922 and abolition of the Caliphate in 1924 were separate events.

## 3. Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922)

### 3.1 Meaning and objectives

Non-Cooperation meant the organised, non-violent withdrawal of Indians' cooperation from British institutions and administration.

Gandhi argued that British rule depended substantially on Indian cooperation. If Indians collectively withdrew that cooperation, colonial rule would become difficult to sustain.

The principal objectives were:

- Attainment of Swaraj.
- Redress of the Punjab wrongs, particularly the Jallianwala Bagh massacre and subsequent repression.
- Redress of the Khilafat wrongs.
- Mobilisation of Indians through non-violent political action.

Gandhi believed Swaraj could be achieved within a year if non-cooperation was sufficiently widespread and disciplined.

### 3.2 Launch and Congress approval

| Event                                 | Date                | Significance                                                            |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Beginning of Non-Cooperation campaign | 1 August 1920       | Gandhi initiated non-cooperation; surrendered British-conferred honours |
| Special Congress Session, Calcutta    | September 1920      | Approved the Non-Cooperation resolution                                 |
| Congress Session, Nagpur              | December 1920       | Confirmed the programme and reorganised Congress                        |
| Mass Non-Cooperation–Khilafat phase   | January 1921 onward | Wider participation across towns and countryside                        |
| Withdrawal                            | February 1922       | Following the Chauri Chaura incident                                    |

The distinction between the August 1920 initiation, September approval, December confirmation and January 1921 mass phase is important.

### 3.3 Calcutta Special Session, September 1920

- President: Lala Lajpat Rai.
- The Congress approved Gandhi's proposal for non-violent non-cooperation.
- The resolution linked the demand for Swaraj with the Punjab and Khilafat grievances.
- Important leaders initially expressed reservations, particularly about boycotting legislative councils and the risk of violence.
- The programme was subsequently confirmed at Nagpur.

### 3.4 Nagpur Congress Session, December 1920

- President: C. Vijayaraghavachariar.
- Confirmed the Non-Cooperation programme.
- Adopted the attainment of Swaraj by peaceful and legitimate means as the Congress objective.
- Reorganised Congress to facilitate wider popular participation.

Important organisational changes:

| Reform                                                                  | Significance                                        |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Provincial Congress Committees reorganised on linguistic lines          | Facilitated organisation through regional languages |
| Congress membership opened more widely, with a four-anna membership fee | Enabled wider participation                         |
| Congress Working Committee of 15 members                                | Provided a smaller executive body                   |
| All India Congress Committee reorganised with 350 members               | Strengthened representative organisation            |
| Local organisational network expanded                                   | Supported mobilisation at village and town levels   |

Prelims distinction: The September 1920 Calcutta session approved Non-Cooperation; the December 1920 Nagpur session confirmed it and reorganised Congress.

## 4. Programme of the Non-Cooperation Movement

### 4.1 Boycott programme

The movement called for withdrawal from institutions supporting colonial rule.

| Area                  | Programme                                                                         |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Titles and honours    | Surrender British-conferred titles and honours                                    |
| Legislative councils  | Boycott elections and council participation                                       |
| Education             | Boycott government and government-aided educational institutions                  |
| Law courts            | Lawyers to withdraw from British courts; encourage alternative dispute settlement |
| Government employment | Encourage withdrawal from colonial services                                       |
| Foreign goods         | Boycott foreign cloth and promote indigenous products                             |
| Official ceremonies   | Boycott government functions and ceremonies                                       |

The programme envisaged progressively stronger non-violent resistance, potentially including civil disobedience if conditions permitted. It did not mean that nationwide tax refusal was implemented from the outset.

### 4.2 Constructive programme

The movement also encouraged the development of Indian institutions and social reform.

- Promotion of Swadeshi and khadi.
- Spinning using the charkha.
- Establishment of national educational institutions.
- Promotion of Hindu–Muslim unity.
- Campaigns against untouchability.
- Promotion of village and community organisation.
- Encouragement of arbitration and panchayats for dispute settlement.

Distinction: Boycott weakened dependence on colonial institutions, while constructive activities aimed to create indigenous alternatives.

### 4.3 Important national educational institutions

| Institution                 | Location                       | Association                                        |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Gujarat Vidyapith (1920)    | Ahmedabad                      | Established by Gandhi                              |
| Jamia Millia Islamia (1920) | Initially Aligarh; later Delhi | Founded during the nationalist and Khilafat period |
| Kashi Vidyapith (1921)      | Banaras                        | National education                                 |
| Bihar Vidyapeeth (1921)     | Patna                          | National education                                 |

These institutions provided alternatives to government-controlled education.

Prelims fact: **Gandhi presided over the Indian National Congress only once, at the Belgaum Session in 1924**.

## 6. Spread and Participation

### 6.1 Urban areas

- Students left government educational institutions.
- Lawyers such as C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru gave up legal practice.
- Foreign cloth was boycotted.
- Merchants and traders participated in Swadeshi campaigns.
- Picketing of liquor shops and shops selling foreign cloth took place.

Limitation: The educational boycott was less successful in some areas because national institutions could not immediately accommodate all students. The court boycott was also uneven.

### 6.2 Peasant participation in Awadh

- Peasants in Awadh faced high rents, illegal cesses, forced labour (begar) and insecurity of tenure.
- Baba Ramchandra became an important organiser of peasant agitation.
- Awadh Kisan Sabha was established in 1920, associated with Jawaharlal Nehru, Baba Ramchandra and other leaders.
- Peasant protests included demands for rent reduction, abolition of begar and action against oppressive landlords.

Some local movements developed methods that differed from Gandhi's non-violent programme.

### 6.3 Eka Movement (1921–1922)

- Mainly associated with parts of the United Provinces, including Hardoi, Bahraich and Sitapur.
- Grievances included high rents and exploitation by landlords and intermediaries.
- Madari Pasi emerged as a prominent leader.
- The movement had links with the wider atmosphere of peasant mobilisation but developed its own organisation and demands.

![Eka Movement](images/eka_movement.png)

### 6.4 Moplah or Mappila Rebellion (1921)

| Feature           | Fact                                                               |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Year              | 1921                                                               |
| Region            | Malabar, Madras Presidency (present-day Kerala)                    |
| Community         | Mappila Muslims                                                    |
| Important leaders | Ali Musaliar and Variyamkunnath Kunjahammad Haji                   |
| Background        | Agrarian grievances, Khilafat mobilisation and colonial repression |

- The rebellion arose in a region marked by longstanding agrarian tensions between tenants and landlords.
- Khilafat and Non-Cooperation mobilisation contributed to the political atmosphere.
- The rebellion developed into armed conflict, including attacks on colonial authority and episodes of communal violence.
- British suppression was severe.

Prelims distinction: The Moplah Rebellion was not simply an implementation of Gandhi's non-violent Non-Cooperation programme.

![Mapilla Rebellion](images/mapilla_rebillion.png)

### 6.5 Tribal movement in the Gudem Hills

- Leader: Alluri Sitarama Raju.
- Region: Gudem Hills, Godavari Agency, Madras Presidency.
- Period: 1922–1924.
- Tribal grievances included restrictions on forest use, interference with customary livelihoods and forced labour.
- Raju admired aspects of Gandhi's nationalist message but advocated armed resistance and led attacks on police stations.
- He was captured and killed in 1924.

Prelims distinction: The Gudem rebellion was an armed tribal movement, not a non-violent campaign authorised by Gandhi.

![Allri Sita Rama Raju](images/allri_sita_rama_raju.png)

### 6.6 Plantation workers in Assam

- Plantation workers faced restrictions on movement and employment.
- Many understood Swaraj as freedom to leave plantations and return to their villages.
- During the Non-Cooperation period, some workers left plantations, but many were prevented from reaching their destinations.

The movement therefore acquired different meanings among different social groups.

## 7. Important Developments During 1921

### 7.1 Tilak Swaraj Fund

- Established in memory of Bal Gangadhar Tilak, who died on 1 August 1920.
- Congress sought to collect ₹1 crore for nationalist activities.
- The 1921 organisational drive also aimed at enrolling one crore Congress members and distributing 20 lakh charkhas.

These were organisational targets, not amounts or numbers necessarily achieved in full.

### 7.2 Boycott of the Prince of Wales

- The Prince of Wales, later King Edward VIII, visited India in November 1921.
- Nationalists organised hartals and boycotts of official receptions.
- The visit was accompanied by protests and, in some places, violence.

### 7.3 Ahmedabad Congress Session, 1921

- President: **Hakim Ajmal Khan**, who presided **in place of the imprisoned president-elect C.R. Das**.
- Congress reaffirmed the Non-Cooperation programme.
- Gandhi was entrusted with extensive authority to direct the movement.

## 8. Chauri Chaura Incident and Withdrawal (1922)

### 8.1 Chauri Chaura incident

| Feature                 | Fact                                                                          |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Date                    | 4 February 1922                                                               |
| Place                   | Chauri Chaura, Gorakhpur district                                             |
| Province                | United Provinces                                                              |
| Event                   | Protesters set fire to a police station following a confrontation with police |
| Police personnel killed | 22                                                                            |
| Consequence             | Gandhi decided to suspend the movement                                        |

During the confrontation, a crowd attacked and burned the police station, killing 22 policemen.

### 8.2 Why Gandhi withdrew the movement

- Gandhi regarded non-violence as essential to Satyagraha.
- The incident convinced him that the mass movement lacked sufficient non-violent discipline.
- He feared that continued escalation would produce further violence.
- He therefore decided to suspend the mass civil-disobedience programme and emphasise constructive activities.

![Chauri Chaura Incident](images/chauri_chaura.png)

### 8.3 Bardoli Resolution, February 1922

- Gandhi had been preparing to launch mass civil disobedience, including a no-tax campaign, in Bardoli, Gujarat.
- Following Chauri Chaura, the Congress Working Committee met at Bardoli.
- On 12 February 1922, it resolved to suspend the proposed mass civil-disobedience campaign and the aggressive phase of Non-Cooperation.
- Emphasis shifted to khadi, communal unity, removal of untouchability and other constructive work.

Important distinction: Bardoli in February 1922 was associated with the decision to suspend mass civil disobedience. The Bardoli Satyagraha of 1928, led by Vallabhbhai Patel, was a separate peasant movement.

## 9. Reactions to Withdrawal

- Several nationalist leaders were disappointed because the movement had achieved substantial momentum.
- Subhas Chandra Bose strongly criticised the withdrawal.
- Gandhi maintained that political success could not justify abandoning non-violence.
- The suspension intensified debate within Congress about the future strategy of the national movement.

### Gandhi's arrest

- Gandhi was arrested on 10 March 1922.
- He was **tried on charges of sedition**.
- He was sentenced to six years' imprisonment.
- He was released in February 1924 on health grounds, before completing the sentence.

## 10. Consequences and Significance

### 10.1 Major achievements

- Transformed Congress into a broader mass political organisation.
- Expanded nationalist participation among peasants, workers, students, traders and sections of women.
- Popularised khadi, Swadeshi and boycott as political instruments.
- Encouraged the establishment of national educational institutions.
- Challenged the legitimacy of colonial institutions through organised withdrawal of cooperation.
- Created a period of significant Hindu–Muslim political cooperation through the Khilafat alliance.

### 10.2 Limitations

- Swaraj was not achieved during the movement.
- Participation varied across regions and social groups.
- Boycotts of government employment, schools and courts were uneven.
- Local struggles sometimes departed from Congress discipline.
- Violence led to the suspension of the movement.
- Hindu–Muslim unity proved difficult to sustain after the movement declined and political circumstances changed.

## 11. Swaraj Party and No-Changers (1922–1930)

### 11.1 Background

- Following the suspension of the Non-Cooperation Movement in February 1922 and Gandhi's arrest in March 1922, Congress leaders differed over their future political strategy.
- **Swarajists (Pro-Changers)** advocated entering legislative councils to oppose British policies from within.
- **No-Changers** favoured continuing the council boycott, constructive work and preparation for future mass movements.

### 11.2 Swarajists vs No-Changers

| Feature | Swarajists (Pro-Changers) | No-Changers |
|---|---|---|
| Principal leaders | C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru, Hakim Ajmal Khan | C. Rajagopalachari, Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad, M.A. Ansari |
| Legislative councils | Favoured entry | Opposed entry |
| Political strategy | Obstruct colonial administration from within councils | Continue constructive work and prepare for civil disobedience |
| Objective | Advance the nationalist struggle through legislative opposition | Strengthen mass organisation outside colonial institutions |

Both groups remained committed to Swaraj and sought to avoid a permanent split in Congress.

### 11.3 Gaya Congress Session (December 1922)

- **President:** C.R. Das.
- The proposal to enter legislative councils was defeated.
- C.R. Das resigned as Congress president.
- C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru subsequently established the **Congress-Khilafat Swarajya Party in January 1923**.

| Position | Leader |
|---|---|
| President | C.R. Das |
| Secretary | Motilal Nehru |

### 11.4 Objectives and Strategy of the Swaraj Party

The Swarajists adopted the strategy of **"end or mend"** legislative councils.

- Contest elections and enter colonial legislative councils.
- Expose the limitations of the Government of India Act, 1919.
- Demand responsible government and greater political rights.
- Oppose government measures and budgetary proposals.
- Create legislative deadlocks where necessary to demonstrate the inadequacy of colonial constitutional reforms.

**Prelims distinction:** The Swarajists advocated council entry as a means of opposing colonial rule, not as acceptance of British authority.

### 11.5 Delhi Compromise (September 1923)

- Congress permitted Swarajists to contest legislative elections while remaining within the organisation.
- Swarajists continued to accept the broader Congress programme, except for the boycott of legislative councils.

### 11.6 Legislative Elections of 1923

- Swarajists won **42 elected seats in the Central Legislative Assembly**.
- They secured a majority in the provincial legislature of the Central Provinces.
- They cooperated with other elected groups and independent members to oppose government proposals.

### 11.7 Gandhi and the Swarajists

- Gandhi initially opposed legislative council entry.
- After his release from prison in February 1924, he gradually reconciled with the Swarajists.
- At the **Belgaum Congress Session in December 1924**, their legislative activities were accepted as part of the Congress programme.
- **Gandhi presided over the Belgaum Session of 1924**, the only Congress session over which he presided.

### 11.8 Achievements

1. Opposed government proposals and budgetary grants within legislatures.
2. Exposed the limitations of the Montagu–Chelmsford constitutional reforms.
3. Raised questions concerning self-government and civil liberties.
4. **Vithalbhai Patel was elected President of the Central Legislative Assembly in 1925.**
5. Helped sustain nationalist political activity during the period between major mass movements.

### 11.9 Decline

- The death of **C.R. Das in 1925** weakened the party.
- Internal differences emerged between Responsivists and Non-Responsivists.
- Communal tensions and disagreements over social and economic issues weakened cooperation.
- The main Swarajist leadership withdrew from legislatures in March 1926.
- The party's remaining legislative activity ended with the renewed mass struggle associated with the Civil Disobedience Movement in 1930.

**Responsivists:** Leaders such as **Lala Lajpat Rai, Madan Mohan Malaviya and N.C. Kelkar** favoured cooperation with the government and acceptance of office under certain circumstances.

### 11.10 Constructive Work of the No-Changers

The No-Changers concentrated on:

- Promotion of khadi and charkha.
- Establishment of national schools and colleges.
- Campaigns against untouchability.
- Hindu–Muslim unity.
- Boycott of foreign cloth and liquor.
- Village organisation and social service.

Their constructive activities helped maintain Congress organisation and prepared workers for subsequent mass movements.

## 12. Khilafat Movement vs Non-Cooperation Movement

| Feature             | Khilafat Movement                                                 | Non-Cooperation Movement                                 |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Main period         | 1919–1924                                                         | 1920–1922                                                |
| Principal grievance | British and Allied policy towards the Ottoman Caliphate           | Colonial rule, Punjab repression and Khilafat grievances |
| Principal leaders   | Ali brothers and other Khilafat leaders                           | Mahatma Gandhi and Congress leaders                      |
| Political scope     | Began around a religious-political issue concerning the Caliphate | Broad Indian anti-colonial mass movement                 |
| Relationship        | Supported joint non-cooperation against British policy            | Incorporated the Khilafat cause into its mobilisation    |
| Major turning point | Turkish political changes and abolition of Caliphate              | Chauri Chaura and suspension in February 1922            |

The movements were closely linked but were not identical. The Non-Cooperation Movement ended in 1922, while the Caliphate itself was abolished in 1924.

## Part I: Khilafat Movement

### Q1. Consider the following statements regarding the Khilafat Movement:

1. It emerged from concerns about the future of the Ottoman Caliphate following the First World War.
2. The Ottoman Empire fought alongside Britain and France during the First World War.
3. Mahatma Gandhi supported the Khilafat Movement and sought to connect it with the Indian nationalist struggle.

A. 1 and 2 only\
B. 2 and 3 only\
C. 1, 2 and 3\
