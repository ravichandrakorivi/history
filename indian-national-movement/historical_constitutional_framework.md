# The Company Rule (1773-1858)

---

# Regulating Act, 1773

The **Regulating Act, 1773** was the first major step by the British government, through Parliament, to control and regulate the East India Company’s administration in India.

Its three principal constitutional contributions were:

1. Greater **British governmental supervision** of Company administration.
2. Recognition of the Company’s **political and administrative functions**, alongside its commercial activities.
3. Foundations of **central administration in India**.

**British Prime Minister at the time:** Lord North.

---

## 1. Historical Background

### A. Battle of Plassey, 1757

**Date:** 23 June 1757.

| Side | Principal leader |
|---|---|
| East India Company | **Robert Clive** |
| Nawab of Bengal | **Siraj-ud-Daulah** |

**Other important participants:**

- **Mir Jafar:** A commander in the Nawab’s army who conspired with the Company and withheld effective support during the battle.
- **Rai Durlabh:** A senior commander whose forces remained largely inactive.
- **Jagat Seth banking house:** Supported the conspiracy against Siraj-ud-Daulah.
- A small **French contingent** supported the Nawab.

**Outcome and significance:**

- Siraj-ud-Daulah was defeated.
- **Mir Jafar became Nawab of Bengal** with Company support.
- The Company obtained substantial financial rewards and political influence.
- The victory depended heavily on conspiracy and the non-participation of important sections of the Nawab’s army.

> **Prelims distinction:** Plassey established the Company’s decisive political influence in Bengal. It did not itself grant Diwani rights.

### B. Battle of Buxar, 1764

**Date:** 22 October 1764.

The Company’s army, commanded by **Hector Munro**, defeated the combined forces of:

| Opponent | Position |
|---|---|
| **Mir Qasim** | Former Nawab of Bengal |
| **Shuja-ud-Daula** | Nawab of Awadh |
| **Shah Alam II** | Mughal Emperor |

**Outcome and significance:**

- Confirmed the Company’s military superiority over a major alliance of Indian powers.
- Consolidated its position in Bengal and northern India.
- Prepared the ground for the **Allahabad settlement of 1765** and acquisition of Diwani rights.

> **Plassey:** Robert Clive versus Siraj-ud-Daulah.  
> **Buxar:** Hector Munro versus Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula and Shah Alam II.

### C. Allahabad Settlement and Diwani Rights, 1765

Following Buxar, **Robert Clive** negotiated the Allahabad settlement.

**Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II** granted the Company the **Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa** in 1765.

**Diwani included:**

- Revenue administration.
- Responsibilities connected with civil justice.

**Significance:** The Company acquired a formal basis for revenue authority and access to substantial territorial income.

> **Prelims trap:** Diwani rights were granted by **Shah Alam II**, not by Siraj-ud-Daulah or Mir Jafar.

### D. Dual Government in Bengal, 1765–1772

**Introduced by:** Robert Clive.

Dual Government divided governmental functions between the **Company** and the **Nawab of Bengal**.

| Function | Meaning | Authority under the arrangement |
|---|---|---|
| **Diwani** | Revenue administration and civil justice | Company |
| **Nizamat** | General administration, policing and criminal justice | Nominally the Nawab |

#### How Did It Work?

- The Company controlled revenue resources through its Diwani rights.
- Revenue administration initially continued through Indian officials acting under Company authority.
- The Nawab nominally remained responsible for administration and maintaining order.
- However, he lacked independent financial resources and effective power.
- The Company exercised decisive influence while avoiding direct responsibility for much of the day-to-day administration.

**Consequences:**

- Separation of effective power from administrative responsibility.
- Weak accountability.
- Revenue exploitation and administrative mismanagement.

> **Core idea:** The Company possessed effective power and financial control, while the Nawab retained nominal administrative responsibility.

#### Abolition

**Warren Hastings abolished Dual Government in 1772** and brought revenue administration under more direct Company management.

> **Introduced:** Robert Clive, 1765.  
> **Abolished:** Warren Hastings, 1772.  
> It was **not abolished by the Regulating Act, 1773**.

### E. Why Was Parliamentary Intervention Needed?

The Company had become a territorial power, but its commercial organisation lacked adequate arrangements for governmental accountability.

Major problems included:

- **Corruption and private enrichment** among Company officials.
- **Revenue exploitation and administrative mismanagement**.
- The **Bengal famine of 1770**, which aggravated economic distress.
- A **Company financial crisis**, leading it to seek British governmental assistance.
- Inadequate coordination among the presidencies.

The British Parliament therefore intervened through the **Regulating Act, 1773**.

---

## 2. Principal Provisions

### A. Governor-General of Bengal

- The **Governor of Bengal** was designated the **Governor-General of Bengal**.
- **Warren Hastings** became the first Governor-General of Bengal.
- The new arrangement began functioning in **1774**.
- The Governor-General and Council exercised civil and military authority over Bengal and administered the Company’s territorial revenues in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.

**Warren Hastings’ tenure as Governor-General of Bengal:** 1774–1785.

> **Office created in 1773:** Governor-General of **Bengal**.  
> **Office created under the Charter Act, 1833:** Governor-General of **India**.

### B. Executive Council of Four Members

The Governor-General was assisted by **four councillors**.

Thus:

**Governor-General + four councillors = five-person decision-making body.**

#### Appointment

The Act named **Warren Hastings and the first four councillors**:

- John Clavering
- George Monson
- Richard Barwell
- Philip Francis

Subsequent appointments were to be made by the Company under the statutory framework.

#### Decision-making

- Decisions followed the **majority of those present**.
- The Governor-General had an ordinary vote.
- In a tie, he had an additional **casting vote**.
- He was bound by the majority and could not generally override it.

**Example:** A three-to-two decision against the Governor-General remained binding on him.

A tie could arise when a member was absent or a seat was vacant.

> **Casting vote ≠ power to override a majority.**

**Practical difficulty:** Conflicts between Warren Hastings and his councillors frequently obstructed administration.

### C. Supervision over Bombay and Madras

- The governments of **Bombay and Madras** were placed under the supervisory authority of the **Governor-General-in-Council at Bengal**.
- This supervision was especially important in **war, peace and treaties with Indian powers**.
- Their freedom to declare war or negotiate treaties independently was restricted.
- Exceptions included imminent necessity and special orders from Company authorities in Britain.
- They retained substantial authority in other administrative matters.

**Significance:** The Act introduced a more centralised administrative framework.

> It began centralisation; it did not establish complete administrative centralisation.

### D. Supreme Court at Calcutta

| Feature | Fact |
|---|---|
| Enabling legislation | **Regulating Act, 1773** |
| Establishment | **Royal Charter, 1774** |
| Location | Fort William, Calcutta |
| Composition | **One Chief Justice and three other judges** |
| First Chief Justice | **Sir Elijah Impey** |
| Appointment of judges | British Crown |
| Powers | Included civil and criminal jurisdiction |

The Court’s jurisdiction covered specified persons and matters under the Act and Charter, including British subjects, certain persons connected with Company service and inhabitants of Calcutta.

Its authority over Company officials, revenue administration and Indians outside Calcutta became disputed.

> **Date distinction:** Act passed in **1773**; Supreme Court established in **1774**.

> The Court did not have unrestricted jurisdiction over every person throughout all Company territories.

### E. Restrictions on Official Conduct

The Act attempted to curb:

- Acceptance of **presents, gifts and bribes**.
- **Private trading activities** that conflicted with official responsibilities.

**Standard textbook formulation:** Company servants were prohibited from private trade and accepting presents or bribes.

**Precision note:** The detailed restrictions varied by office and activity. Express private-trade prohibitions covered the Governor-General, councillors and Supreme Court judges.

**Purpose:** To prevent officials from using public authority for private financial gain.

### F. Court of Directors

The **Court of Directors** was the Company’s governing body in Britain.

The Act reformed its arrangements:

- **Term of office:** Four years.
- **Retirement:** One-fourth annually.

This provided greater continuity in management.

> The Act reformed the **existing Court of Directors**; it did not create it.

### G. Reporting to the British Government

The Court of Directors was required to submit correspondence concerning:

1. **Revenue affairs**
2. **Civil administration**
3. **Military administration**

This increased the British government’s access to information and strengthened supervision over Company administration.

## 3. Checks and Balances—and Their Problems

| Arrangement | Intended check | Practical difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| Majority rule in the Council | Restrain the Governor-General’s individual authority | Governor-General could be outvoted; executive conflict |
| Supreme Court | Judicial scrutiny within its jurisdiction | Disputes over jurisdiction and official acts |
| Bengal’s supervision of Bombay and Madras | Coordinate policies | Unclear boundaries and ineffective supervision |
| Reporting to the British government | Increase accountability | Governmental oversight remained inadequate |

**Three principal defects:**

1. Conflict between the **Governor-General and Council**.
2. Conflict between the **Supreme Court and Company administration**.
3. Incomplete control over **Bombay and Madras**.

> The Act introduced checks and balances, but did not establish a clear modern separation of powers.

---

## 4. Constitutional Significance

- **Governmental regulation:** Established a framework for British supervision of Company administration.
- **Recognition of political functions:** Acknowledged that the Company governed territories as well as conducting trade.
- **Central administration:** Created a superior executive authority at Bengal and subordinated other major presidencies in important matters.
- **Institutional development:** Provided for the Governor-General-in-Council and Supreme Court.
- **Accountability:** Introduced restrictions on official conduct and reporting obligations.

### What the Act Did Not Do

- It did **not** end Company government.
- It did **not** introduce direct Crown rule.
- It did **not** create the Governor-General of India.
- It did **not** establish the Board of Control.
- It did **not** introduce representative government.

---

## 5. Corrective Legislation: Act of Settlement, 1781

Also called the **Amending Act, 1781**.

It addressed judicial difficulties arising from the earlier arrangements by:

- Protecting the Governor-General and Council regarding acts performed in their official capacity.
- Providing protection for Company servants regarding specified official acts.
- Excluding **revenue matters** from the Supreme Court’s jurisdiction.
- Clarifying judicial–executive relations.
- Providing for respect for Hindu and Muslim personal laws in relevant matters.

> **1773:** New administrative and judicial framework.  
> **1781:** Correction of important jurisdictional difficulties.

---

## 6. Regulating Act, 1773 vs Pitt’s India Act, 1784

| Feature | Regulating Act, 1773 | Pitt’s India Act, 1784 |
|---|---|---|
| Main contribution | Initial framework of governmental regulation and central administration | Stronger governmental supervision of political affairs |
| Governor-General’s councillors | **Four** | **Three** |
| Important institutional change | Governor-General of Bengal and provision for Supreme Court | **Board of Control** |
| Supervision from Britain | Reporting obligations and oversight | More systematic supervision of civil, military and revenue affairs |
| Company government | Continued | Continued under stronger supervision |

### Two Different “Dual” Arrangements

| Arrangement | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Dual Government in Bengal, 1765–1772** | Company held Diwani; Nawab nominally retained Nizamat |
| **Dual control under Pitt’s India Act, 1784** | Court of Directors retained commercial and administrative functions; Board of Control supervised political affairs |

# Amending Act, 1781

The **Amending Act, 1781**, also known as the **Act of Settlement** or **Bengal Judicature Act**, was passed by the British Parliament to address defects in the judicial arrangements established under the **Regulating Act, 1773**.

**Governor-General of Bengal at the time:** Warren Hastings.

> **Central purpose:** To clarify the Supreme Court’s jurisdiction and reduce conflict between the Court and Company administration.

---

## 1. Background

The Regulating Act, 1773, provided for the Supreme Court at Calcutta, which was established in **1774**.

However, the boundaries between the Court’s authority and that of the Governor-General-in-Council were insufficiently defined.

Disputes arose concerning:

- Judicial scrutiny of **official acts**.
- Interference in **revenue administration**.
- Jurisdiction over Indians outside Calcutta.
- Application of English law to Indians.
- The relationship between the Supreme Court and the Company’s courts.

These difficulties prompted the **Amending Act, 1781**.

---

## 2. Principal Provisions

### A. Protection for Official Acts

The Act exempted the **Governor-General and Council** from the Supreme Court’s jurisdiction for acts performed in their official capacity.

Company servants were similarly protected regarding specified official acts.

> **Prelims distinction:** This concerned official acts; it was not blanket immunity for every private act of an official.

### B. Revenue Matters Excluded

The Act excluded **revenue matters** and acts performed in revenue collection under the applicable regulations from the Supreme Court’s jurisdiction.

**Purpose:** To prevent judicial interference from obstructing the Company’s revenue administration.

> **1773 arrangements:** Disputes over judicial interference in revenue administration.  
> **1781 Act:** Revenue matters expressly excluded from the Supreme Court’s jurisdiction.

### C. Jurisdiction over Inhabitants of Calcutta

The Act affirmed the Supreme Court’s jurisdiction over **inhabitants of Calcutta**, including Indians.

However, a person outside Calcutta did not become subject to the Court’s jurisdiction merely because that person:

- Owned land.
- Paid land revenue.
- Was connected with revenue collection in the ways specified by the Act.

> **Important:** Do not interpret the Act as making the Supreme Court a court exclusively for Europeans, or as removing every form of its jurisdiction outside Calcutta.

### D. Application of Personal Laws

In specified civil matters, the Court was to apply:

- **Hindu law to Hindus**.
- **Muslim law to Muslims**.

This was particularly relevant to matters such as **inheritance and succession**, with provision also made for applicable laws and usages in dealings between parties.

The Act required respect for Indian religious practices and usages.

> **Prelims distinction:** It did not require the uniform application of English law to all Indians in every matter.

### E. Appeals from Provincial Courts

Appeals from the **provincial courts** were to be dealt with by the **Governor-General-in-Council**, rather than the Supreme Court.

This recognised the Company’s judicial system outside Calcutta and clarified its relationship with the Crown-established Supreme Court.

### F. Regulations for Provincial Courts

The Act empowered the **Governor-General-in-Council** to frame regulations for the provincial courts and councils.

These regulations did not require registration in the Supreme Court.

**Significance:** It strengthened the Company administration’s authority over its provincial judicial arrangements.

---

## 3. Two Judicial Arrangements: Important Distinction

| Feature | Supreme Court at Calcutta | Company’s provincial courts |
|---|---|---|
| Institutional basis | Crown-established court under the 1773 Act and 1774 Charter | Company’s judicial arrangements |
| Position after the 1781 Act | Jurisdiction clarified and restricted in important respects | Authority of the Governor-General-in-Council recognised |
| Revenue administration | Revenue matters excluded from its jurisdiction | Continued within the Company’s administrative and judicial framework |
| Appeals from provincial courts | Not made the ordinary appellate authority over these courts | Appeals lay to the Governor-General-in-Council |

> The 1781 Act clarified the coexistence of the **Supreme Court** and the **Company’s judicial system**.

---

## 4. Regulating Act, 1773 vs Amending Act, 1781

| Feature | Regulating Act, 1773 | Amending Act, 1781 |
|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | Regulate Company administration | Correct judicial difficulties under the earlier arrangements |
| Supreme Court | Authorised its establishment | Clarified and restricted its jurisdiction |
| Official acts | Scope of judicial scrutiny became disputed | Protection provided for specified official acts |
| Revenue matters | Jurisdictional conflict arose | Excluded from Supreme Court jurisdiction |
| Indian personal laws | Application of law became a source of difficulty | Express provision for applicable Hindu and Muslim laws |
| Provincial judicial administration | Relationship with Supreme Court insufficiently clear | Governor-General-in-Council’s authority strengthened |

---

## 5. Important Prelims Traps

| Incorrect Statement | Correct position |
|---|---|
| The Amending Act established the Supreme Court at Calcutta. | The Court was established in 1774 under authority provided by the Regulating Act, 1773. |
| The Act abolished the Supreme Court. | It clarified and restricted its jurisdiction. |
| It brought revenue matters under the Supreme Court. | Revenue matters were excluded. |
| It granted officials immunity for all private and official conduct. | **Incorrect:** The protection concerned specified official acts. |
| The Supreme Court could hear cases only involving Europeans. | Its jurisdiction included Indian inhabitants of Calcutta. |
| Appeals from provincial courts were transferred to the Supreme Court. | They were to be dealt with by the Governor-General-in-Council. |
| It established the Board of Control. | The Board was established by Pitt’s India Act, 1784. |
| It introduced uniform English law for Hindus and Muslims. | It provided for their applicable personal laws in specified matters. |

---

## 6. Quick Revision

**Amending Act, 1781:**

1. Also called the **Act of Settlement**.
2. Corrected judicial difficulties following the **Regulating Act, 1773**.
3. Protected specified **official acts**.
4. Excluded **revenue matters** from Supreme Court jurisdiction.
5. Affirmed jurisdiction over **inhabitants of Calcutta**.
6. Recognised applicable **Hindu and Muslim personal laws**.
7. Placed provincial court appeals with the **Governor-General-in-Council**.
8. Empowered the Governor-General-in-Council to regulate provincial courts.

# Pitt’s India Act, 1784

**Pitt’s India Act, 1784**, also known as the **East India Company Act, 1784**, strengthened British governmental control over the East India Company’s administration in India.

It was named after **William Pitt the Younger**, the British Prime Minister.

**Governor-General of Bengal at the time:** Warren Hastings.

Its central features were:

1. Distinction between the Company’s **commercial and political functions**.
2. Establishment of the **Board of Control**.
3. Introduction of **dual control**, also called **double government**.
4. Stronger supervision over the Company’s civil, military and revenue affairs.

---

## 1. Historical Background

### A. Limitations of the Regulating Act, 1773

The Regulating Act began governmental supervision but left several problems:

- Conflict between the Governor-General and his Council.
- Disputes between the Supreme Court and Company administration.
- Inadequate control over Bombay and Madras.
- Insufficient British governmental supervision of Company affairs.

### B. Amending Act, 1781

The **Amending Act, 1781**, addressed important judicial difficulties, particularly the Supreme Court’s jurisdiction.

However, it did not comprehensively reorganise British governmental control over the Company’s political administration.

### C. Pitt’s India Act, 1784

The 1784 Act created a stronger framework for political supervision while retaining the Company’s commercial organisation and administrative machinery.

> **1773:** Initial framework of regulation.  
> **1781:** Correction of judicial difficulties.  
> **1784:** Stronger governmental control over political administration.

---

## 2. Distinction between Commercial and Political Functions

The Act distinguished between the Company’s two broad functions:

| Function | Meaning | Principal authority |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial** | Trade and business operations | **Court of Directors** |
| **Political** | Territorial government, civil and military administration, and revenue affairs | **Board of Control**, exercising governmental supervision |

The Company continued to govern through its existing machinery, but its political administration became subject to stronger British governmental direction.

> **Important:** This was a division of responsibilities within a system of supervision. It did not remove the Court of Directors from every administrative function.

---

## 3. Board of Control

### A. Establishment and Composition

The Act established a new body commonly called the **Board of Control**.

Its formal designation was the **Board of Commissioners for the Affairs of India**.

The Board was constituted on a six-member basis:

- **Chancellor of the Exchequer**
- **A Secretary of State**
- **Four other Privy Councillors**

The commissioners were appointed by the **British Crown**.

**Terms to understand:**

- **Chancellor of the Exchequer:** Senior British minister responsible for financial affairs.
- **Privy Council:** A body of advisers to the British monarch.

> The Secretary of State included here was not the later **Secretary of State for India**, whose office was created in **1858**.

### B. Powers

The Board could supervise, direct and control operations relating to:

1. **Civil government**
2. **Military government**
3. **Revenues of the British possessions in India**

It could examine and revise the Company’s governmental correspondence and directions to India.

**Effect:** The British government acquired supreme supervisory authority over the Company’s political administration.

### C. Despatches and Instructions

Company despatches concerning matters within the Board’s authority were subject to its scrutiny and approval.

The Board could:

- Approve proposed instructions.
- Modify instructions.
- Require instructions to be prepared and transmitted.

> Avoid interpreting “all despatches” as giving the Board ordinary management of the Company’s purely commercial business.

### D. Secret Committee

A **Secret Committee of three directors** provided a channel for transmitting confidential political instructions, particularly concerning war, peace and diplomacy.

This allowed sensitive instructions from the Board to be transmitted without disclosure to the full Court of Directors.

> **Prelims distinction:** The Secret Committee consisted of Company directors; it was different from the Board of Control.

---

## 4. Court of Directors

The Court of Directors continued as the Company’s governing body.

It retained:

- Management of **commercial affairs**.
- Important administrative functions.
- Substantial appointment and patronage powers, subject to statutory restrictions.

Its political and administrative decisions were subject to the Board’s supervisory authority.

> Pitt’s India Act did not abolish or replace the Court of Directors.

---

## 5. Dual Control or Double Government

The Act established a system involving two authorities in Britain:

| Authority | Represented | Main role |
|---|---|---|
| **Court of Directors** | East India Company | Commercial management and continuing administrative functions |
| **Board of Control** | British government | Supervision and direction of political, civil, military and revenue affairs |

### How Did It Work?

- The Company retained its organisation and officials.
- The Court of Directors continued conducting Company business.
- The British government, through the Board, exercised superior control over political administration.

**This arrangement continued, with later modifications, until 1858.**

> Dual control did not mean that the Board and Court possessed equal authority over political policy. The Board held superior supervisory authority in its assigned sphere.

### Distinguish the Two “Dual” Arrangements

| Feature | Dual Government in Bengal | Dual Control under Pitt’s India Act |
|---|---|---|
| Introduced | **1765** | **1784** |
| Associated with | **Robert Clive** | **William Pitt the Younger** |
| Authorities | Company and Nawab of Bengal | Court of Directors and Board of Control |
| Division | Diwani and Nizamat | Commercial management and governmental supervision |
| End | **1772**, under Warren Hastings | **1858**, with the transfer to Crown government |

---

## 6. Changes in Administration in India

### A. Governor-General’s Council

The Governor-General’s councillors were reduced from **four to three**, with the **Commander-in-Chief** included in the arrangement.

Thus:

**Governor-General + three councillors = four-person body.**

The Governor-General retained a **casting vote**.

### Why Did This Strengthen His Position?

With all four persons participating:

- The Governor-General and one councillor could produce a **two-to-two tie**.
- The Governor-General could then use his casting vote.

However, he could still be outvoted by all three councillors.

> Reduction of the Council did not itself give the Governor-General a general power to override an adverse majority.

### B. Stronger Control over Bombay and Madras

The Act strengthened the Governor-General-in-Council’s control over the subordinate presidencies.

This was particularly important in:

- War and peace.
- Diplomacy and treaties.
- Matters involving the application of territorial revenues.

> **1773:** Introduced supervisory subordination.  
> **1784:** Strengthened and made that control more effective.

---

## 7. Restrictions on War and Expansion

The Act declared that pursuing schemes of conquest and territorial extension was contrary to the policy of the British nation.

It restricted the initiation of wars and certain treaties without the required authorisation, while allowing specified exceptions, including responses to actual or imminent attack.

**Purpose:** To restrain costly and unauthorised military adventures.

**Historical outcome:** These restrictions did not prevent subsequent British territorial expansion.

---

## 8. “British Possessions in India”

A standard constitutional-history distinction associated with the Act is that the Company’s territories were, for the first time, described as **“British possessions in India.”**

### Significance

- Emphasised the British state’s interest and authority in those territories.
- Reinforced governmental supremacy over the Company’s political administration.

### What It Did Not Mean

- Company government did not end in 1784.
- Direct Crown rule did not begin in 1784.
- The Company continued trading and administering territories.

> **Direct Crown rule began under the Government of India Act, 1858.**

Descriptions of the Company as a “subordinate department of the State” express its increased political subordination. They should not be read as saying that the Company legally ceased to exist.

---

## 9. Important Distinction: Act of 1786

Do not attribute the following later changes to Pitt’s India Act, 1784.

The **Act of 1786**, associated with the appointment of **Lord Cornwallis**, permitted:

1. The Governor-General to override his Council in specified extraordinary circumstances, assuming responsibility for the decision.
2. The offices of **Governor-General and Commander-in-Chief** to be held by the same person.

| Provision | Associated Act |
|---|---|
| Council reduced from four councillors to three | **1784** |
| Board of Control established | **1784** |
| Power to override the Council in specified extraordinary circumstances | **1786** |
| Combination of Governor-General and Commander-in-Chief offices | **1786** |

---

## 10. Comparison of the Three Acts

| Feature | Regulating Act, 1773 | Amending Act, 1781 | Pitt’s India Act, 1784 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main concern | Regulation of Company administration | Judicial–executive conflict | Governmental control over political administration |
| Key institutional feature | Governor-General of Bengal and provision for Supreme Court | Clarification of Supreme Court jurisdiction | Board of Control |
| Governor-General’s councillors | Four | No corresponding reduction | Three |
| Revenue affairs | Reporting and administrative arrangements | Excluded from Supreme Court jurisdiction | Subject to Board supervision |
| Major contribution | Foundations of central administration | Correction of judicial difficulties | Dual control and stronger governmental supremacy |

---

## 11. Important Prelims Traps

| Statement | Correct position |
|---|---|
| Pitt’s India Act created the Court of Directors. | **Incorrect:** It already existed. |
| The Board of Control managed the Company’s ordinary commercial business. | **Incorrect:** Commercial management remained with the Court of Directors. |
| Revenue affairs were outside the Board’s authority. | **Incorrect:** Its powers expressly covered revenue affairs. |
| The Board and Court were institutions located in India. | **Incorrect:** Both operated in Britain. |
| The Governor-General’s Council was reduced to three councillors. | **Correct.** |
| Three councillors meant a three-person body including the Governor-General. | **Incorrect:** There were four persons in total. |
| The Act introduced direct Crown rule. | **Incorrect:** Company government continued until 1858. |
| The Act created the Governor-General of India. | **Incorrect:** That change came under the Charter Act, 1833. |
| The 1784 Act gave a general power to override the Council. | **Incorrect:** The specified extraordinary override power belongs to the 1786 legislation. |
| Restrictions on aggressive wars stopped British expansion. | **Incorrect:** Expansion continued. |

---

## 12. Quick Revision

- **Prime Minister:** William Pitt the Younger.
- **Governor-General at enactment:** Warren Hastings.
- **New institution:** Board of Control.
- **Board composition:** Six-member arrangement.
- **Board’s sphere:** Civil, military and revenue affairs.
- **Court of Directors:** Commercial management and continuing administrative functions.
- **System:** Dual control or double government.
- **Secret Committee:** Three directors.
- **Governor-General’s councillors:** Reduced from four to three.
- **Presidencies:** Stronger subordination of Bombay and Madras.
- **Territorial description:** “British possessions in India.”
- **War policy:** Restrictions on aggressive wars and unauthorised treaties.
- **Direct Crown rule:** Began in 1858, not 1784.

# Charter Act, 1793

The **Charter Act, 1793**, also known as the **East India Company Act, 1793**, renewed the Company’s charter and continued its **trade monopoly for another 20 years**.

It largely consolidated the existing system of Company government under British governmental supervision.

---

## 1. Background

| Earlier Act | Principal contribution |
|---|---|
| **Regulating Act, 1773** | Governor-General of Bengal, Executive Council and provision for Supreme Court |
| **Amending Act, 1781** | Clarification of judicial jurisdiction |
| **Pitt’s India Act, 1784** | Board of Control and dual control |
| **Act of 1786** | Exceptional Council-override power and permission to combine the offices of Governor-General and Commander-in-Chief |

The Charter Act, 1793, continued the Company’s privileges while strengthening and clarifying this administrative framework.

### Meaning of Charter Renewal

A charter renewal continued the Company’s legally authorised privileges and responsibilities for a further period, subject to conditions imposed by Parliament.

> **1793 renewed the Company’s trade monopoly; 1813 substantially ended it.**

---

## 2. Principal Provisions

### A. Trade Monopoly Continued for 20 Years

The Company’s exclusive trading privileges were continued for **another 20 years**, until the next renewal in **1813**.

The Company also continued administering its Indian territories.

> The Act did not end either the Company’s commercial activities or Company government.

### B. Permission for Licensed Private Trade

The Company could grant licences allowing **private individuals and Company employees** to undertake authorised trade.

**Important distinction:**

- The Company retained its trading monopoly.
- Private trading could occur under permissions granted within that framework.
- Such licences did not establish a general right to unrestricted free trade.

> **Continuation of monopoly and permission for licensed private trade could coexist.**

### C. Council-Override Power Extended

The exceptional power to override the Council, associated with **Cornwallis and the Act of 1786**, was continued and extended to **future Governors-General and Governors of the presidencies**.

**Meaning:**

- The executive head could act against the Council’s majority in specified circumstances.
- This involved responsibility for the decision.
- It did not make every Council decision merely advisory.

| Power | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Casting vote** | Resolves an equality of votes |
| **Override power** | Permits acting against an adverse majority in specified circumstances |

> **1786:** Override power associated with Cornwallis.  
> **1793:** Its continuation and extension to future Governors-General and Governors.

### D. Greater Authority over Bombay and Madras

The Governor-General received greater authority over the governments of **Bombay and Madras**.

When present in either presidency, he could exercise authority superior to that of its Governor.

**Significance:** This further strengthened administrative centralisation.

### E. Board of Control Paid from Indian Revenues

The salaries and expenses of the paid members and staff of the **Board of Control** were charged to **Indian revenues**.

**Why this matters:**

- The Board functioned in **Britain**.
- It supervised the Company’s political administration in India.
- Its costs were borne from Indian revenues.

> **Board of Control established:** Pitt’s India Act, 1784.  
> **Payment from Indian revenues:** Charter Act, 1793.

### F. Royal Approval of Senior Appointments

Royal approval was required for the appointment of:

1. **Governor-General**
2. **Governors**
3. **Commander-in-Chief**

> Requiring royal approval did not mean that Company government had been replaced by direct Crown rule.

### G. Departure from India without Permission

Senior Company officials were prohibited from leaving India without permission.

Unauthorised departure was treated as **resignation from office**.

### H. Commander-in-Chief’s Council Membership

The **Commander-in-Chief was not automatically a member of the Governor-General’s Council**.

He could become a member if **specially appointed by the Court of Directors**.

> Holding military command and holding a Council seat were distinct positions.

### I. Arrangements during the Governor-General’s Absence

When the Governor-General was absent from Bengal, a **Vice-President** could be appointed from among the civilian members of his Council to act in his place there.

This maintained administration while the Governor-General exercised responsibilities elsewhere.

---

## 3. Continuation of Dual Control

The Act retained the framework established by Pitt’s India Act, 1784.

| Institution | Principal role |
|---|---|
| **Court of Directors** | Commercial management and continuing administrative functions |
| **Board of Control** | British governmental supervision of political, civil, military and revenue affairs |

The Act did not abolish either institution.

Company government continued under British governmental supervision.

> The Charter Act, 1793, was principally a measure of **renewal and consolidation**.

---

## 4. Distinguish Three Developments of 1793

| Development | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Charter Act, 1793** | Parliamentary legislation renewing Company privileges and regulating administration |
| **Permanent Settlement, 1793** | Revenue settlement introduced under Cornwallis, fixing the government’s land-revenue demand on zamindars in the areas covered |
| **Cornwallis Code, 1793** | Body of administrative and judicial regulations associated with Cornwallis |

> Do not treat every reform introduced under Cornwallis in 1793 as a provision of the Charter Act.

---

| **Charter Act, 1813** | Ended the monopoly over Indian trade, while retaining exceptions for **trade in tea and trade with China** |
| **Charter Act, 1833** | Ended the Company’s commercial activities altogether |

> **1793:** Monopoly renewed.  
> **1813:** Monopoly substantially ended, with specified exceptions.  
> **1833:** Commercial activities ended.

# Charter Act, 1813

The **Charter Act, 1813**, also known as the **East India Company Act, 1813**, renewed the Company’s territorial administration for another **20 years**, but ended its monopoly over trade with India, subject to two important exceptions:

1. **Trade in tea**
2. **Trade with China**

It also made important provisions concerning **education, Christian missionaries and British governmental supervision**.

---

## 1. Historical Background

### A. Expiry of the Earlier Charter

The **Charter Act, 1793**, had renewed the Company’s commercial privileges for 20 years.

Their renewal in 1813 provided Parliament an opportunity to reconsider the Company’s commercial and administrative position.

### B. Pressure from British Business Interests

British merchants and manufacturers wanted access to Indian markets without the Company’s monopoly restrictions.

Their demands were encouraged by:

- Expansion of British manufacturing during the **Industrial Revolution**.
- The search for markets for British manufactured goods.
- Growing support for **free trade** and opposition to exclusive commercial privileges.

**Laissez-faire** broadly means allowing economic activity to operate with limited governmental interference.

### C. Napoleon’s Continental System

Napoleon’s **Continental System**, introduced in 1806, sought to exclude British trade from continental European markets.

The resulting restrictions increased British merchants’ interest in alternative markets, including India.

### D. Missionary Pressure

Christian missionary groups pressed for greater freedom to enter Company territories and undertake religious and educational activities.

---

## 2. Principal Provisions

### A. End of the Company’s Monopoly over Indian Trade

The Act opened trade with India to other **British subjects**, subject to prescribed regulations and licensing requirements.

However, the Company retained its monopolies over:

- **Trade in tea**
- **Trade with China**

### Understand the Two Exceptions Separately

| Exception | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Tea trade** | A commodity-based exception |
| **Trade with China** | A geographical exception covering trade with China |

> Do not reduce the provision to “only tea trade with China remained a monopoly.” The standard formulation contains two distinct exceptions.

### What Changed—and What Continued?

- Other British merchants could participate more widely in Indian trade.
- The Company continued commercial operations.
- Its remaining monopolies and commercial activities ended under the **Charter Act, 1833**.

> **Loss of monopoly did not mean immediate termination of the Company’s trading activities.**

### B. Company Government Continued for 20 Years

The Company retained the administration of its territories and revenues for another **20 years**.

This continuation was expressly subject to the **sovereignty of the British Crown**.

| Aspect | Position under the Act |
|---|---|
| Territorial administration | Continued with the Company |
| Ultimate sovereignty | Expressly affirmed as belonging to the Crown |
| Direct Crown government | Not introduced |

> Recognition of Crown sovereignty in 1813 did not mean the beginning of direct Crown rule. Direct Crown government began in **1858**.

### C. Annual Provision for Education

The Act provided for an annual sum of **not less than ₹1 lakh** for:

1. Revival and improvement of literature.
2. Encouragement of learned Indians.
3. Introduction and promotion of knowledge of the sciences.

This provision is associated with **Section 43** of the Act.

**Significance:** It marked an important legislative recognition that education in India should receive support from public revenues.

### What the Education Provision Did Not Establish

- It did not introduce universal compulsory education.
- It did not restrict the entire allocation exclusively to English education.
- It did not settle the later debate between supporters of Oriental learning and Western education.

> **1813:** Statutory provision for educational expenditure.  
> **1835:** Macaulay’s Minute and Bentinck’s decision favouring English education.  
> **1854:** Wood’s Despatch.

### D. Entry of Christian Missionaries

The Act permitted Christian missionaries to enter British territories in India, subject to the applicable authorisation arrangements, and undertake religious and educational activities.

**Significance:**

- Reduced the earlier restrictions imposed by the Company.
- Facilitated the expansion of missionary activity and missionary education.

> Christian missionaries had operated in India before 1813. The Act did not mark the first arrival of Christianity or Christian missionaries in India.

### E. Separate Commercial and Territorial Accounts

The Company was required to maintain separate accounts for:

- **Commercial transactions**
- **Territorial revenues and expenditure**

**Purpose:** To distinguish the finances of trading operations from those of territorial administration and improve financial scrutiny.



### F. Stronger Board of Control Supervision

The powers of the **Board of Control** were clarified and enlarged.

It continued supervising the Company’s governmental affairs, including civil, military and revenue administration.

The existing **dual-control framework** remained:

| Institution | Principal role |
|---|---|
| **Court of Directors** | Company management, commercial operations and continuing administrative functions |
| **Board of Control** | British governmental supervision of political administration |

> The Board of Control was established in **1784**, not in 1813.

### G. Regulations Laid before Parliament

Regulations made by the governments of **Bengal, Madras and Bombay** were required to be laid before the British Parliament.

**Significance:** This strengthened parliamentary oversight.

> Parliamentary oversight of regulations and the declaration of Crown sovereignty were separate provisions.

### H. Taxation Powers

The Act empowered local governments to impose taxes on persons within their jurisdiction and provide penalties for non-payment.

> “Local governments” here refers to colonial governmental authorities; it should not be read as the introduction of modern elected local self-government.

---

## 3. Supplementary Financial Fact: Dividend

The Company’s shareholders were entitled to an annual dividend of **10½ per cent on the Company’s capital stock** under the financial arrangements.

---

## 4. Constitutional and Economic Significance

| Development | Significance |
|---|---|
| Opening of Indian trade | Reduced the Company’s exclusive commercial privileges and widened access for British merchants |
| Crown sovereignty affirmed | Clarified the Company’s subordinate constitutional position |
| Education allocation | Recognised a claim of education upon public revenues |
| Missionary entry | Facilitated religious and educational activity |
| Separate accounts | Distinguished commercial finances from territorial finances |
| Stronger oversight | Increased scrutiny by the Board of Control and Parliament |

The Company remained both a **trading organisation** and a **territorial administrator** after 1813.

---

## 5. Charter Acts: Essential Comparison

| Feature | Charter Act, 1793 | Charter Act, 1813 | Charter Act, 1833 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Company’s trade | Monopoly continued for 20 years | Indian trade opened, except tea trade and trade with China | Commercial activities ended |
| Territorial administration | Continued | Continued for another 20 years | Continued |
| Education | No comparable ₹1 lakh provision | Annual provision of at least ₹1 lakh | Not the origin of the 1813 education provision |
| Governor-General’s title | Governor-General of Bengal | Governor-General of Bengal | Governor-General of India |

> **1793:** Monopoly renewed.  
> **1813:** Monopoly substantially ended, with two exceptions.  
> **1833:** Company ceased to function as a commercial body.

---

| Statement | Correct position |
|---|---|

| Affirmation of Crown sovereignty introduced direct Crown rule. | **Incorrect:** Direct Crown government began in 1858. |

---

## 7. Quick Revision

**Charter Act, 1813:**

1. Ended the Company’s monopoly over Indian trade.
2. Retained **tea trade** and **trade with China** as exceptions.
3. Continued Company territorial administration for **20 years**.
4. Expressly affirmed **Crown sovereignty**.
5. Provided **at least ₹1 lakh annually for education**.
6. Permitted greater access for **Christian missionaries**.
7. Required separate **commercial and territorial accounts**.
8. Strengthened **Board of Control supervision**.
9. Required government regulations to be laid before **Parliament**.
10. Provided taxation and enforcement powers to local governments.

# Charter Act, 1833

The **Charter Act, 1833**, also known as the **Government of India Act, 1833**, was a major constitutional landmark in British India.

It was the **final step towards administrative and legislative centralisation** before the Government of India Act, 1858.

Its most important consequences were:

1. Creation of the office of the **Governor-General of India**.
2. Establishment of a central legislative authority for the whole of British India.
3. Ending of the East India Company’s commercial activities.
4. Beginning of systematic codification of Indian laws.
5. Declaration that Indians should not be excluded from public employment on grounds of religion, birth, descent or colour.

---

## 1. Historical Background

### A. Charter Act, 1813

The Charter Act, 1813:

- Ended the Company’s monopoly over Indian trade.
- Retained its monopoly over tea trade and trade with China.
- Continued Company administration for another 20 years.
- Provided for an annual allocation of at least ₹1 lakh for education.
- Allowed Christian missionaries to enter India.

### B. Need for Further Reform

By 1833:

- The Company’s commercial role had become increasingly difficult to justify.
- Its main importance lay in territorial administration.
- Separate presidencies created administrative and legislative differences.
- British merchants demanded complete freedom of trade.
- Parliament sought a more centralised and uniform system of government.

The Charter Act, 1833, therefore reorganised the Company’s political, administrative and commercial position.

---

## 2. Principal Provisions

### A. Governor-General of India

The Act redesignated the **Governor-General of Bengal** as the **Governor-General of India**.

- **Lord William Bentinck** became the first Governor-General of India.
- The Governor-General acquired authority over all British territories in India.
- He exercised control over civil and military administration.
- The governments of Bengal, Madras, Bombay and other territories were placed under his overall authority.

> **Important distinction:**
>
> - **First Governor-General of Bengal:** Warren Hastings, under the Regulating Act, 1773.
> - **First Governor-General of India:** Lord William Bentinck, under the Charter Act, 1833.
> - **First Viceroy of India:** Lord Canning, under the Government of India Act, 1858.

### B. Centralisation of Civil and Military Administration

The Governor-General-in-Council received authority to:

- Superintend civil administration.
- Control military affairs.
- Direct the affairs of the Company throughout British India.
- Exercise authority over the subordinate presidencies.

This made the Governor-General-in-Council the central executive authority in India.

### C. Centralisation of Financial Administration

The Act strengthened financial centralisation:

- Revenues were to be raised under the authority of the Governor-General-in-Council.
- The Governor-General-in-Council exercised greater control over expenditure.
- The financial administration of the presidencies was brought under central supervision.

> This did not create a modern unified budgetary system, but it strengthened central control over revenues and expenditure.

---

## 3. Centralisation of Legislative Power

### A. Exclusive Legislative Authority

The Act vested the **Governor-General-in-Council** with exclusive legislative power for the whole of British India.

This included the power to make laws for:

- Bengal
- Madras
- Bombay
- The North-Western Provinces
- Other British territories in India

### B. Legislative Powers of Bombay and Madras Removed

The governments of Bombay and Madras were deprived of their independent legislative powers.

They could:

- Propose legislative measures.
- Send their proposals to the Governor-General-in-Council.

They could not independently enact laws for their presidencies.

> **Prelims distinction:**
>
> - Under the earlier system, Bombay and Madras possessed separate legislative authority.
> - The Charter Act, 1833, centralised legislative authority in the Governor-General-in-Council.

### C. Regulations and Acts

- Laws made under earlier arrangements were generally known as **Regulations**.
- Laws made by the Governor-General-in-Council under the new central legislative authority were called **Acts**.

This distinction is associated with the central legislative system created by the Charter Act, 1833.

---

## 4. Addition of the Law Member

The Act added a **fourth member** to the Governor-General’s Council.

This member was known as the **Law Member**.

### Features

- The Law Member was mainly associated with legislative work.
- He could participate in the Council when legislative matters were being considered.
- He provided professional legal advice for law-making.

**First Law Member:** **Thomas Babington Macaulay**.

> **Prelims trap:** The Law Member was added by the **Charter Act, 1833**, not by the Charter Act, 1853.

---

## 5. Law Commission and Codification

The Act provided for the codification and consolidation of Indian laws.

- The **First Law Commission** was established in **1834**.
- **Macaulay** became its first chairman.
- The Commission worked towards the systematic codification of laws in India.

### Long-Term Significance

The codification process later contributed to major legal codes, including:

- Indian Penal Code, enacted in 1860.
- Civil and criminal procedure codes developed later.

> The Act initiated the process of systematic codification; it did not itself enact the Indian Penal Code.

---

## 6. End of the Company’s Commercial Activities

The Act ended the East India Company’s remaining commercial functions.

This included the end of its monopoly over:

- Tea trade.
- Trade with China.

After the Act:

- The Company ceased to function as a commercial body.
- It became a purely administrative and political organisation.
- Its territories and revenues continued to be held and administered on behalf of the British Crown.

> **Important sequence:**
>
> - **1793:** Company’s trade monopoly renewed.
> - **1813:** Indian trade opened, except tea trade and trade with China.
> - **1833:** All Company commercial activities ended.
> - **1858:** Company government ended.

---

## 7. Territories Held in Trust for the Crown

The Act declared that the Company’s territories in India were held:

> **“In trust for His Majesty, His heirs and successors.”**

### Significance

- It emphasised that the Company did not possess Indian territories as an independent sovereign.
- It reinforced the constitutional supremacy of the British Crown.
- It clarified the subordinate position of the Company.

### What It Did Not Mean

- Direct Crown rule did not begin in 1833.
- The Company continued to administer Indian territories.
- Direct Crown administration began only in **1858**.

---

## 8. Free Trade and European Immigration

The Act removed remaining restrictions on:

- European immigration into India.
- Acquisition of property by Europeans in India.
- Private European trade and settlement, subject to applicable regulations.

### Significance

- It facilitated greater European commercial activity.
- It encouraged European settlement and investment.
- It paved the way for deeper economic penetration and colonial expansion.

> The removal of restrictions did not mean that every European received unrestricted legal freedom in all circumstances. Regulations continued to apply.

---

## 9. Civil-Service Provision

The Act declared that Indians should not be excluded from Company employment on grounds of:

- Religion
- Birth
- Descent
- Colour
- Place of birth

It attempted to introduce the principle of **open competition** for recruitment to the Company’s civil services.

### Practical Limitation

The provision was opposed by the Court of Directors and was not effectively implemented.

The real introduction of competitive recruitment came later through the **Charter Act, 1853** and subsequent developments.

> **Prelims distinction:**
>
> - **1833:** Principle of non-discrimination and intended open competition.
> - **1853:** Open competitive recruitment was formally provided for.

---

## 10. Slavery

The Act directed the administration to take steps towards:

- Improving the condition of slaves.
- Gradually abolishing slavery in India.

Slavery was abolished in British India in **1843**.

> **Prelims caution:** The Act initiated governmental attention towards the issue; abolition occurred later, in 1843.

---

## 11. Why Is the Act Important?

The Charter Act, 1833, is important because it:

1. Created the office of the **Governor-General of India**.
2. Established a central government for all British territories in India.
3. Centralised legislative authority.
4. Removed the legislative powers of Bombay and Madras.
5. Ended the Company’s commercial activities.
6. Initiated codification of Indian laws.
7. Added the Law Member to the Governor-General’s Council.
8. Declared that Indians should not be excluded from public employment on discriminatory grounds.
9. Affirmed that Company territories were held in trust for the Crown.
10. Began the process of removing restrictions on European settlement and property ownership.

---

# Charter Act, 1853

The **Charter Act, 1853** was the last of the Charter Acts passed between **1793 and 1853**.

It was an important constitutional landmark because it:

1. Separated the **legislative and executive functions** of the Governor-General’s Council.
2. Introduced **open competitive recruitment** for the civil services.
3. Introduced **local representation** in the central legislature.
4. Continued Company rule without specifying any fixed period.
5. Further weakened the Company’s control over appointments and administration.

---

## 1. Historical Background

The earlier Charter Acts had gradually transformed the East India Company:

| Act | Principal development |
|---|---|
| **Charter Act, 1793** | Company’s trade monopoly renewed for 20 years |
| **Charter Act, 1813** | Indian trade opened, except tea and China trade |
| **Charter Act, 1833** | Company’s commercial activities ended; Governor-General of India created |
| **Charter Act, 1853** | Legislative and executive functions separated; civil-service recruitment opened to competition |

By 1853:

- The Company was no longer a commercial organisation.
- It functioned mainly as an administrative authority.
- Its legislative machinery needed reorganisation.
- The demand for greater efficiency and merit-based recruitment was increasing.
- Parliament was no longer willing to renew the Company’s charter for a fixed period.

---

## 2. No Fixed Period for Company Rule

The Act continued the Company’s possession of Indian territories on behalf of the British Crown.

However, unlike the earlier Charter Acts, it did **not** renew the Company’s rule for a specific period such as 20 years.

### Significance

- The Company could continue to govern India only until Parliament decided otherwise.
- This indicated that the Company’s rule had become temporary and conditional.
- It showed the growing possibility of transferring Indian administration directly to the Crown.

> The Act did not end Company rule. Direct Crown rule began under the Government of India Act, 1858.

---

## 3. Separation of Executive and Legislative Functions

This was the most important provision of the Act.

Before 1853, the Governor-General’s Council performed both:

- Executive functions
- Legislative functions

The Act separated these functions for the first time.

### A. Legislative Wing

Six additional members were added to the Governor-General’s Council for legislative purposes.

The legislative body came to be known as the:

- **Indian Legislative Council**
- **Central Legislative Council**

The legislative wing functioned as a separate deliberative body and followed procedures resembling those of the British Parliament.

### B. Legislative Council as a Separate Body

During legislative proceedings:

- The Council functioned as a law-making body.
- Bills could be discussed and debated.
- Legislative procedures were distinguished from executive administration.
- The legislative process became a specialised function requiring separate machinery.

> It was a step towards a legislature, but it was not a democratic or representative legislature in the modern sense.

### C. Composition and Local Representation

The additional legislative members included representatives connected with the governments of:

- Madras
- Bombay
- Bengal
- Agra

This introduced **local or provincial representation** into the central legislative machinery for the first time.

> This was representation of governments and administrative units, not elected representation of the people.

### D. Governor-General’s Powers

A law passed by the Legislative Council required the assent of the Governor-General.

The Governor-General could:

- Assent to a Bill.
- Refuse assent.
- Veto a Bill.

Therefore, the Legislative Council did not possess complete legislative independence.

---

## 4. Law Member Became a Full Member

Under the Charter Act, 1833, a Law Member had been added to the Governor-General’s Council mainly for legislative work.

The Charter Act, 1853:

- Made the Law Member a **full member of the Governor-General’s Executive Council**.
- Allowed the Law Member to participate in both executive and legislative functions.

> **1833:** Law Member added mainly for legislative work.  
> **1853:** Law Member became a full member of the Executive Council.

---

## 5. Open Competition for Civil Services

The Act ended the exclusive patronage of the Court of Directors over appointments to the Company’s civil services.

It introduced the principle of:

> **Open competitive examination for recruitment to the civil services.**

### Main Features

- The covenanted civil service was opened to competition.
- Indians were theoretically eligible to compete.
- Recruitment was to be based on merit rather than nomination or patronage.
- The system weakened the Court of Directors’ control over appointments.

### Macaulay Committee

The **Macaulay Committee** was appointed in **1854** to design the system of competitive recruitment.

It was associated with:

- Thomas Babington Macaulay.
- The development of competitive recruitment for the Indian Civil Service.

> **Prelims distinction:**
>
> - **Charter Act, 1833:** Stated that Indians should not be excluded from public employment on discriminatory grounds; the provision was not effectively implemented.
> - **Charter Act, 1853:** Introduced open competitive recruitment.
> - **Macaulay Committee:** Appointed in 1854 to develop the examination system.

### Practical Limitation

Although Indians were legally eligible to compete, the examination was held in England and the age and educational requirements made participation difficult.

Therefore, formal legal eligibility did not immediately result in significant Indian representation in the higher civil services.

---

## 6. Court of Directors

The strength of the Court of Directors was reduced:

- From **24 members to 18 members**.
- Six members were nominated by the British Crown.

This weakened the Company’s institutional independence and reduced the importance of its patronage over appointments.

> The Court of Directors continued to exist until the transfer of power to the Crown in 1858.

---

## 7. Administrative Centralisation

The Act further strengthened the central government in India.

The Governor-General-in-Council continued to exercise authority over:

- Civil administration
- Military administration
- Revenue administration
- Legislative affairs

The subordinate presidencies remained under the control of the central government.

---

## 8. Constitutional Significance

The Charter Act, 1853, was significant because it:

1. Created a separate legislative wing of the Governor-General’s Council.
2. Separated executive and legislative functions for the first time.
3. Introduced local representation in the central legislative machinery.
4. Introduced open competitive recruitment to the civil services.
5. Reduced the patronage of the Court of Directors.
6. Made the Law Member a full member of the Executive Council.
7. Indicated that Company rule could be terminated at any time by Parliament.
8. Further strengthened centralisation of British administration.

---

## 9. What the Act Did Not Do

The Act did not:

- End Company rule immediately.
- Introduce direct Crown rule.
- Introduce responsible government.
- Establish an elected legislature.
- Make the Legislative Council independent of the Governor-General.
- Immediately create equal practical access for Indians to the civil services.
- Abolish the Court of Directors.

---

| Feature | Charter Act, 1833 | Charter Act, 1853 |

| Governor-General | Created Governor-General of India | Continued the central office |

| Court of Directors | Continued | Strength reduced from 24 to 18 |
| Company rule | Continued | Continued without a fixed renewal period |
| Commercial role | Ended under the 1833 Act | Company remained an administrative body |

# The Crown Rule (1858-1947)

---

# Government of India Act, 1858

## 1. Background

- The **Revolt of 1857** was the immediate background to the Act.
- It exposed serious weaknesses in the East India Company’s administration and prompted the British Parliament to transfer the government of India to the **British Crown**.
- The **Charter Act, 1853** had already continued the Company’s government **without granting another fixed 20-year term**, leaving Parliament free to terminate it.

### Name and date

- **Official title:** *An Act for the Better Government of India*.
- Commonly described in textbooks as the **Act for the Good Government of India**.
- Received royal assent on **2 August 1858**. [legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/21-22/106/pdfs/ukpga_18580106_en.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

## 2. End of Company Rule and Beginning of Crown Rule

The Act transferred the Company’s **governmental powers, territories and revenues** to the British Crown.

- India was henceforth to be governed **by and in the name of Queen Victoria**.
- The East India Company ceased to govern India.
- This marked the beginning of **direct Crown rule**, which continued until 1947.

### Important distinction: Company rule versus Company’s existence

| Event | Year |
|---|---|
| Company ceased to function as a commercial body | **1833** |
| Company’s rule in India ended | **1858** |
| Company was formally dissolved | **1874** |

> **1858 abolished Company rule—not the Company’s corporate existence immediately.**

Therefore, the textbook expression “abolished the East India Company” should be understood as **ending its role as the governing authority**. [UK Parliament](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Lords/1873-04-29/debates/992845f4-139a-4d7d-9119-fdb7cec2f7ce/SecondReading?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

## 3. Governor-General and Viceroy

Under Crown rule, the Governor-General also came to be known as the **Viceroy**, representing the British Crown in India.

- **Lord Canning** became the **first Viceroy of India**.
- He was also the **last Governor-General under Company rule**.

### Wording to remember carefully

The office of **Governor-General was not abolished**. The same person served as **Viceroy and Governor-General**.

- **Governor-General:** Head of the government in India.
- **Viceroy:** Representative of the British Crown.

Queen Victoria’s Proclamation expressly referred to Canning as the first **Viceroy and Governor-General**. [Wikisource, the free online library](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Proclamation_by_the_Queen_in_Council%2C_to_the_princes%2C_chiefs%2C_and_people_of_India?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### Frequently confused personalities

| Office | First holder |
|---|---|
| Governor-General of Bengal | **Warren Hastings** |
| Governor-General of India | **Lord William Bentinck** |
| Viceroy of India | **Lord Canning** |

## 4. End of the System of Double Government

The Act abolished the existing machinery of control through the:

- **Board of Control**
- **Court of Directors**

It therefore ended the **system of double government established by Pitt’s India Act, 1784**.

Their governmental powers were transferred to the new **Secretary of State for India**.

### Do not confuse these systems

| System | Meaning | Relevant period |
|---|---|---|
| **Dual Government in Bengal** | Division of Diwani and Nizamat responsibilities, introduced by Robert Clive | **1765–1772** |
| **Double government under Pitt’s India Act** | Control through the Company’s directors and the British government’s Board of Control | **1784–1858** |
| **Provincial dyarchy** | Division of provincial subjects into reserved and transferred categories | Introduced by the **Government of India Act, 1919** |

> The Act of 1858 ended **Pitt’s system of double government**, not Clive’s Dual Government in Bengal.

---

## 5. Secretary of State for India

The Act created the office of **Secretary of State for India**.

### Position

- A member of the **British Cabinet**.
- Based in **Britain**, not India.
- Ultimately responsible to the **British Parliament**.

### Powers

- Exercised extensive **supervision, direction and control over Indian administration**.
- Took over governmental powers previously exercised through the Company and the Board of Control.
- Became the principal authority in Britain dealing with the government and revenues of India.

### Financial point

The Secretary of State’s salary and the expenses of the India administration in Britain were charged to **Indian revenues**.

> **Secretary of State for India → Britain**  
> **Viceroy and Governor-General → India**

---

## 6. Council of India

The Act established a **15-member Council of India** to assist the Secretary of State.

### Composition and position

| Feature | Provision |
|---|---|
| Location | **London** |
| Members | **15**, apart from the Secretary of State |
| Chairman | **Secretary of State for India** |
| Initial selection | **8 appointed by the Crown; 7 elected by the Court of Directors** |
| General role | Primarily **advisory** |

The initial election of seven members by the outgoing Court of Directors was a **transitional arrangement**; it did not mean that the Court continued as a governing authority.

### Important qualification

For basic revision, remember that the Council was **primarily advisory**. However, saying that it had **no binding powers whatsoever** would be inaccurate: the Act required the concurrence of a majority of the Council for grants or appropriations of Indian revenues.

### Important distinction

The **Council of India in London** was **not**:

- The Governor-General’s Executive Council in India.
- The Indian Legislative Council.
- An elected body representing the Indian population.

---

## 7. Secretary of State-in-Council: Body Corporate

The Act constituted the **Secretary of State-in-Council** as a **body corporate**.

It could:

- **Sue**.
- **Be sued**.

This applied in both **India and England**.

### Meaning

It possessed a legal identity through which proceedings relating to the administration’s rights and liabilities could be brought.

> **Body corporate does not mean an elected legislative body.**

The provisions concerning the Secretary of State, Council, finances and legal proceedings appear in the original Act. [legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/21-22/106/pdfs/ukpga_18580106_en.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

## 8. Nature and Limitations of the Act

The Act primarily reorganised the machinery through which Indian administration was **supervised and controlled from Britain**.

### What changed?

- Company rule was replaced by direct Crown rule.
- The Board of Control and Court of Directors were replaced by the **Secretary of State assisted by the Council of India**.
- British ministerial responsibility for Indian administration became more direct.

### What did not change substantially?

- Much of the existing administrative machinery **within India** continued.
- The Act did **not** introduce:
  - Responsible government accountable to Indians.
  - An elected Indian legislature.
  - Parliamentary democracy in India.
  - Provincial dyarchy.

> **Accountability to the British Parliament did not mean accountability to the Indian people.**

The textbook observation about administrative continuity is useful. However, calling the transfer “merely a formality” understates its constitutional importance.

| Nature | Parliamentary legislation | Royal declaration |

- Existing **treaties and engagements with Indian princes** would be respected.

# Indian Councils Act, 1861

## 1. Historical Background

### Developments preceding the Act

- **Revolt of 1857:** Highlighted the dangers of excluding influential Indians from the colonial administration.
- **Government of India Act, 1858:** Ended Company rule and transferred the government of India to the British Crown.
- **Indian Councils Act, 1861:** Reorganised the executive and legislative councils and enabled the association of Indians with law-making through nomination.

### Viceroy associated with the Act

**Lord Canning**, the first Viceroy of India.

### Main themes

1. Association of non-officials, including Indians, with legislative work.
2. Restoration of legislative powers to Bombay and Madras.
3. Recognition of the portfolio system.
4. Emergency ordinance-making power of the Governor-General.

---

## 2. Expansion of the Central Legislative Council

The Act enlarged the Governor-General’s Council **for legislative purposes**.

### Composition of the additional membership

| Feature | Provision |
|---|---|
| Additional members | **Not fewer than 6 and not more than 12** |
| Method of selection | **Nomination by the Governor-General** |
| Non-official members | **At least half of the additional members** |
| Term of additional members | **Two years** |
| Role | Participation in **legislative business** |

### Important distinctions

- The **6–12 figure refers to additional legislative members**, not the total membership of the council.
- Additional legislative members did **not automatically become members of the Executive Council**.
- The requirement of at least half being non-official applied to the **additional members**, not the entire council.

### What does “non-official” mean?

A non-official member was a person outside the relevant government service.

**Non-official did not necessarily mean Indian.** Europeans outside government service could also be nominated.

> Do not confuse “at least half non-official” with “at least half Indian”.

---

## 3. Association of Indians with Law-Making

The nomination of non-official members opened the way for Indians to participate in legislative proceedings.

### First Indian nominees in 1862

Lord Canning nominated:

1. **Raja of Benaras**
2. **Maharaja of Patiala**
3. **Sir Dinkar Rao**

### Nature of this participation

- Members were **nominated**, not elected.
- Indian nominees largely belonged to **princes, landed elites and other influential sections**.
- They did not represent the population through a democratic electoral mandate.

### Statutory wording versus practical effect

The Act required the nomination of **non-official members**; it did not reserve a specified number of seats exclusively for Indians.

Its practical importance was that **Indians began to be associated with the colonial law-making process**.

> Beginning of representative institutions in a limited sense does not mean the introduction of elected or responsible government.

---

## 4. Restoration of Provincial Legislative Powers

The Act restored legislative powers to the governments of:

- **Bombay**
- **Madras**

These powers had been taken away by the **Charter Act, 1833**, which centralised legislation under the Governor-General in Council.

### Meaning of legislative decentralisation

Provincial governments again obtained machinery for making laws concerning their territories, subject to the prescribed controls of the central and British authorities.

### Comparison

| Act | Direction of change |
|---|---|
| **Charter Act, 1833** | Centralised legislative authority; deprived Bombay and Madras of legislative powers |
| **Indian Councils Act, 1861** | Restored legislative powers to Bombay and Madras; began legislative decentralisation |

### Limit

This did **not** make the provinces autonomous or sovereign.

**Provincial autonomy** is associated with the **Government of India Act, 1935**, implemented in the provinces in **1937**.

---

## 5. Legislative Councils in Other Provinces

The Act provided machinery for extending provincial legislative institutions beyond Bombay and Madras.

### Subsequent developments

| Province | Establishment of legislative council |
|---|---|
| **Bengal** | **1862** |
| **North-Western Provinces and Oudh** | **1886 notification; first meeting in 1887** |
| **Punjab** | **1897** |

### Correction to remember

**North-Western Provinces** and **North-West Frontier Province (NWFP)** were different administrative units.

Do not reproduce the textbook wording **“NWFP—1866”**.

**Higher-priority prelims point:** The Act restored legislative powers to Bombay and Madras and enabled the expansion of provincial legislative councils.

---

## 6. Recognition of the Portfolio System

The Act empowered the Governor-General to make rules for the convenient transaction of executive business.

This gave statutory recognition to the **portfolio system introduced by Lord Canning in 1859**.

### What is a portfolio?

A **portfolio** is responsibility for a particular department or area of government, such as finance, home affairs or law.

### How the system worked

- A member of the Executive Council was placed in charge of one or more departments.
- He handled the business of those departments under the prescribed rules.
- Routine departmental matters could be decided without requiring the entire council to consider every case.
- Important matters could still require consideration by the Governor-General or the council.

### Example

A member responsible for finance would handle financial business, while another member would handle matters relating to law.

### Significance

The system introduced clearer departmental responsibility and more efficient distribution of executive work.

### Important qualification

Although it contributed to the development of cabinet-style departmental organisation, it did **not establish parliamentary cabinet government**.

Members were not ministers collectively responsible to an elected Indian legislature.

> **1859: Canning introduced the portfolio system.**
> **1861: The Act gave it statutory recognition.**

---

## 7. Emergency Ordinance-Making Power

The Act empowered the Governor-General to issue **ordinances in cases of emergency**.

### Main features

- An ordinance could be issued **without the concurrence of the legislative council**.
- It had the **force of law**.
- Its duration was **not more than six months from promulgation**.
- It could cease earlier through disallowance or superseding legislation.

### Meaning of an ordinance

An ordinance is a law issued by the executive under a special law-making power, instead of following the ordinary legislative process.

---

## 8. Powers and Limitations of the Legislative Councils

The councils participated in making laws, but their authority was tightly restricted.

### A. Limited scope of business

- Their role centred on **legislation**.
- They had no general power to question or supervise executive administration.
- They could not hold the executive politically accountable.

### B. Financial restrictions

- They had **no control over the budget**.
- Measures affecting public revenue or expenditure required the prescribed **prior sanction of the Governor-General**.
- They did not possess the later right of general budget discussion associated with the **Indian Councils Act, 1892**.

### C. Executive control over legislation

- Passage by the legislative council alone was insufficient.
- The Governor-General’s **assent** was necessary for central legislation.
- The Crown retained the power of disallowance, communicated through the Secretary of State.

### D. No democratic mandate

- Membership was based on nomination.
- There were no elections under the Act.
- Indian participation was largely confined to elite sections.

---

## 9. Distinguish the Acts of 1853, 1858 and 1861

| Feature | Charter Act, 1853 | Government of India Act, 1858 | Indian Councils Act, 1861 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main development | Separation of legislative and executive functions of the Governor-General’s Council | Transfer from Company rule to Crown rule | Reorganisation and expansion of councils |
| Important institutional change | Additional members for legislative work | Secretary of State and Council of India in London | Nominated non-official participation in legislative councils |
| Provincial legislative powers | Centralised framework continued | No major restoration of provincial legislative powers | Restored to Bombay and Madras |
| Key association | Legislative specialisation | Crown rule | Indian nomination, portfolio system and legislative decentralisation |

### Important distinction

The Act of **1861 did not first separate legislative and executive functions** of the Governor-General’s Council.

That development is associated with the **Charter Act, 1853**.

---

| Statement | Correct position |
|---|---|

| All non-official members had to be Indians. | **Incorrect:** Non-officials could include Europeans. |
| At least half of the additional legislative members were to be non-officials. | **Correct.** |
| It restored legislative powers to Bombay and Madras. | **Correct.** |
| It introduced provincial autonomy. | **Incorrect:** It began legislative decentralisation. |

> **Indian Councils Act, 1861: Nominated Indian participation, 6–12 additional central legislative members, at least half non-official, restoration of Bombay–Madras legislative powers, recognition of the portfolio system, and emergency ordinances lasting up to six months.**

# Indian Councils Act, 1892

## 1. Historical Background

### Earlier development: Indian Councils Act, 1861

The Act of 1861 had associated Indians with legislative work through **nomination**, but the councils had limited functions:

- No general right to question the executive.
- No right to discuss the annual budget.
- No control over the executive government.

### Demand for further reform

The **Indian National Congress, founded in 1885**, demanded:

- Expansion of legislative councils.
- Greater Indian representation.
- Introduction of an elective element.
- Greater opportunities to discuss financial and administrative matters.

The **Indian Councils Act, 1892**, was a limited response to these demands.

**Viceroy at the time:** Lord Lansdowne.

---

## 2. Main Features at a Glance

| Area | Change under the Act of 1892 |
|---|---|
| Council membership | Increased membership of central and provincial legislative councils |
| Representation | Introduced a limited indirect electoral element through recommendations followed by nomination |
| Financial business | Allowed discussion of the annual financial statement, but not voting on it |
| Questions | Allowed questions on matters of public interest, subject to restrictions |
| Executive accountability | Did not establish responsible government |
| Government control | Official predominance continued; official majority was retained at the Centre |

---

## 3. Expansion of Legislative Councils

The Act increased the permitted membership of legislative councils.

### Important numerical provisions

| Council | Membership provision under the Act of 1892 |
|---|---|
| Governor-General’s Legislative Council | **10–16 additional members** |
| Bombay Legislative Council | **8–20 additional members** |
| Madras Legislative Council | **8–20 additional members** |
| Bengal Legislative Council | Maximum of **20 nominated councillors** |
| North-Western Provinces and Oudh Legislative Council | Maximum of **15 nominated councillors** |

### Central Council: comparison with 1861

| Act | Additional legislative members |
|---|---|
| Indian Councils Act, 1861 | **6–12** |
| Indian Councils Act, 1892 | **10–16** |

### Important numerical trap

The figure **10–16** refers to **additional legislative members**.

It does **not** mean:

- The total strength of the Central Legislative Council.
- The number of Indian members.
- The number of non-official members alone.

“Additional member” and “non-official member” are not interchangeable terms.

---

## 4. Limited Introduction of the Electoral Principle

The Act and the regulations made under it introduced a system in which certain bodies recommended persons for nomination to legislative councils.

### How the system worked

1. A recognised body selected or recommended a person.
2. The competent government authority formally nominated that person to the council.

Thus:

- The legal form remained **nomination**.
- The selection process introduced an **indirect electoral element**.

### Was the word “election” used in the Act?

**No.**

The arrangement was described through nomination and the regulations governing it.

Nevertheless, it is commonly described as the **beginning of the elective principle in the colonial legislative councils**.

### Two statements to distinguish

| Statement | Assessment |
|---|---|
| The Act introduced a limited indirect electoral element. | **Correct.** |
| The Act introduced direct popular elections to legislative councils. | **Incorrect.** |

> Avoid saying either “full elections were introduced” or “there was no electoral element whatsoever”.

---

## 5. Who Recommended Members?

### A. Central Legislative Council

Some non-official members were nominated by the **Governor-General/Viceroy** on recommendations from:

- The **non-official members of provincial legislative councils**.
- The **Bengal Chamber of Commerce**.

### B. Provincial Legislative Councils

Recommendations came from bodies and interests such as:

- Municipalities.
- District boards.
- Universities.
- Chambers of commerce.
- Trade associations.
- Zamindars and other landholding interests.

Formal nomination was made by the relevant **Governor or Lieutenant-Governor**.

### Nature of representation

Representation was restricted to selected institutions and influential interests.

It was not based on:

- Universal adult franchise.
- Equal voting rights.
- Direct election by the general population.

### Meaning of “non-official”

A non-official member was a person outside government service.

**Non-official did not necessarily mean Indian, elected or opposed to the government.**

---

## 6. Right to Discuss the Budget

The Act authorised rules permitting discussion of the **annual financial statement**, commonly described as the budget.

Members could:

- Express opinions on financial policy.
- Criticise expenditure and taxation.
- Draw attention to public grievances connected with finances.

### Important limitations

Members could not:

- **Vote on the budget.**
- Approve or reject the annual financial statement.
- Move resolutions on it under the 1892 arrangements.

### Discussion versus control

| Power | Position under the 1892 arrangements |
|---|---|
| Discuss the annual financial statement | **Allowed** |
| Express criticism of financial policy | **Allowed** |
| Vote on the budget | **Not allowed** |
| Compel the government to change expenditure through a budget vote | **Not allowed** |

> **Budget discussion did not mean budgetary control.**

---

## 7. Right to Ask Questions

Members were permitted to ask questions concerning **matters of public interest**.

### Restrictions under the rules

- **Six days’ prior notice** was required.
- **Supplementary questions were not permitted.**
- The presiding authority could disallow questions under the rules.
- A question did not provide an unrestricted opportunity for debate on the government’s answer.

### What is a supplementary question?

A supplementary question is a **follow-up question arising from the answer to the original question**.

**Example:**

- Original question: “How much was spent on famine relief?”
- Supplementary question: “Why was expenditure lower in the worst-affected district?”

The 1892 arrangements permitted the original question, subject to the rules, but did not confer the right to ask such supplementary questions.

### Important comparison

- **1892:** Limited right to ask questions.
- **1909:** Right to ask supplementary questions was introduced, subject to restrictions.

---

## 8. Official Majority and Continuing Government Control

The expansion of councils did not transfer control to Indian representatives.

- An **official majority continued in the Central Legislative Council**.
- Official predominance generally continued in the provinces.
- The executive did not depend on the confidence of the councils.

### Meaning of official majority

An official majority exists when government officials constitute more than half of the council’s membership.

### Accuracy note

Textbooks commonly summarise the 1892 arrangements as retaining official majorities at the Centre and in the provinces.

However, do not convert this into the claim that the Act expressly required an official majority in every provincial council at all times. Provincial practice had exceptions, notably in Bombay.

**Core prelims point:** The Act did not establish elected control or responsible government.

---

## 9. Significance and Limitations

### Significance

1. **Broader representation:** More non-official members could participate.
2. **Indirect electoral element:** Recommendations reduced exclusive reliance on nomination at the government’s discretion.
3. **Financial discussion:** Members could publicly examine the annual financial statement.
4. **Questions to the executive:** Members gained a limited means of obtaining information.
5. **Political platform:** Councils offered opportunities to voice Indian grievances.

### Limitations

1. Representation remained narrow and institution-based.
2. Formal nomination continued.
3. Members could discuss, but not vote on, the budget.
4. Supplementary questions were not allowed.
5. Government control remained strong.
6. The executive was not responsible to the legislature.

> **1892 expanded participation and discussion without establishing parliamentary government.**

---

## 10. Indian Councils Acts, 1861 and 1892: Comparison

| Feature | Act of 1861 | Act of 1892 |
|---|---|---|
| Central additional legislative members | **6–12** | **10–16** |
| Selection | Nomination | Nomination, with recommendations introducing a limited indirect electoral element |
| Indian participation | Began through nomination of non-officials | Expanded |
| General budget discussion | Not provided | Permitted under rules |
| Voting on the budget | Not provided | Not permitted |
| Questions to the executive | No general right provided | Permitted with restrictions |
| Supplementary questions | Not provided | Not permitted |
| Responsible government | Not established | Not established |

---

## 11. UPSC Prelims Traps

| Statement | Correct position |
|---|---|
| The Act was passed before the establishment of the Indian National Congress. | **Incorrect:** Congress was founded in 1885. |
| The Act introduced direct elections. | **Incorrect:** It introduced a limited indirect electoral element through recommendations. |
| The word “election” appeared in the Act. | **Incorrect.** |
| The Central Legislative Council had only 16 members in total. | **Incorrect:** The maximum of 16 referred to additional members. |
| All additional members were non-officials. | **Incorrect.** |
| Members could discuss the budget. | **Correct.** |
| Members could vote on the budget. | **Incorrect.** |
| Members could ask supplementary questions. | **Incorrect:** This development is associated with 1909. |
| The Act introduced separate electorates for Muslims. | **Incorrect:** This is associated with the Indian Councils Act, 1909. |
| The Act made the executive responsible to the legislature. | **Incorrect.** |

### One-line revision

> **1892: Enlarged councils, indirect electoral element through recommendations, budget discussion without voting, and questions with six days’ notice but no supplementary questions.**

| Voting on the budget | Not provided | Not permitted |
| Questions to the executive | No general right provided | Permitted with restrictions |
| Supplementary questions | Not provided | Not permitted |
| Responsible government | Not established | Not established |

---

# Indian Councils Act, 1909 — Morley–Minto Reforms

## 1. Identity and Historical Background

The **Indian Councils Act, 1909**, together with its implementing rules and regulations, is known as the **Morley–Minto Reforms**.

| Person | Office |
|---|---|
| **Lord Morley — John Morley** | Secretary of State for India |
| **Lord Minto II** | Viceroy of India |

### Why were reforms introduced?

The Indian Councils Act, 1892, had provided limited representation, budget discussion and questions to the executive. These concessions did not satisfy demands for greater Indian participation.

The British government sought to accommodate some demands, particularly those of moderate political leaders and influential groups, while retaining control over administration.

### Important preceding events

| Year | Event | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **1905** | Partition of Bengal under Lord Curzon | Intensified opposition to colonial rule |
| **1905 onwards** | Swadeshi and Boycott movements | Expanded political mobilisation |
| **1906** | Simla Deputation | Demanded separate Muslim representation and weightage |
| **1906** | All-India Muslim League founded at Dacca | Organised Muslim political demands |
| **1907** | Surat Split | Split between Moderates and Extremists in the Congress |
| **1909** | Morley–Minto Reforms | Expanded participation while retaining colonial control |

### Simla Deputation

- Led by **Aga Khan III**.
- Met **Lord Minto in 1906**.
- Demanded separate representation for Muslims and representation beyond a strictly population-based allocation.

> **1909 was not the beginning of all representative participation:** nominated Indian participation had begun under the 1861 framework, and a limited indirect electoral element had developed under the 1892 framework.

---

## 2. Expansion of Legislative Councils

The Act enlarged both central and provincial legislative councils.

### Central Legislative Council

| Act | Number of additional legislative members |
|---|---|
| **Indian Councils Act, 1861** | **6–12** |
| **Indian Councils Act, 1892** | **10–16** |
| **Indian Councils Act, 1909** | Maximum of **60** |

### Meaning of “16 to 60”

The familiar textbook statement that membership increased **“from 16 to 60”** refers to the maximum **additional membership**.

The figure **60** does not mean:

- Sixty elected members.
- Sixty Indian members.
- Sixty non-official members.
- The entire council’s strength, including ex-officio members.

### Provincial councils

The size of provincial legislative councils was **not uniform**.

The prescribed limits differed across provinces.

---

## 3. Membership and Electoral Arrangements

The councils included:

- **Ex-officio members:** Members by virtue of holding a specified office.
- **Nominated official members:** Government officials appointed to the council.
- **Nominated non-official members:** Persons outside government service appointed to the council.
- **Elected members:** Persons chosen through the prescribed electoral arrangements.

### Important distinction

**Official/non-official** describes a person’s relationship with government service.

**Nominated/elected** describes the method of entering the council.

Therefore:

> **A non-official member could be nominated or elected.**

Non-official status did not necessarily mean that a member was Indian or opposed to government policy.

### Electoral principle: 1892 versus 1909

| Act | Position |
|---|---|
| **1892** | Certain bodies recommended persons for formal nomination; the Act avoided the word “election” |
| **1909** | The Act explicitly recognised **elected membership alongside nomination** |

### Restricted franchise

The reforms did not establish universal adult franchise.

Electoral eligibility depended on prescribed qualifications connected with property, taxation, status or institutional membership. Many electoral arrangements were indirect.

---

## 4. Official and Non-Official Majorities

| Level | Position under the reforms |
|---|---|
| **Central Legislative Council** | **Official majority retained** |
| **Provincial legislative councils** | **Non-official majorities provided** |

### Why did a provincial non-official majority not establish popular control?

- Non-official members included government nominees.
- A non-official majority did not necessarily mean an elected majority.
- The executive did not depend on the confidence of the legislative council.
- Government retained important powers over legislation and administration.

> **Non-official majority, elected majority and responsible government are three different concepts.**

---

## 5. Separate Electorates for Muslims

The reforms introduced **separate electorates for Muslims**.

### Meaning

For seats assigned to Muslim separate electorates:

- The representatives were Muslim.
- They were elected by **qualified Muslim voters**.
- Non-Muslim voters did not vote for those seats.

### Separate electorate versus reservation in a joint electorate

| Feature | Separate electorate | Reserved seat within a joint electorate |
|---|---|---|
| Candidate for the specified seat | Belongs to the specified community | Belongs to the specified reserved category |
| Voters | Eligible voters of that community | All eligible voters in the constituency |

**The decisive difference is who votes.**

### Example

A seat reserved for a community does not, by itself, mean that only that community votes.

It becomes a **separate electorate** when the electorate choosing the representative is also restricted to that community.

### Significance

The reforms institutionalised **religion-based political representation**.

- Textbooks often describe this as the **“legalisation of communalism”**.
- **Lord Minto** is associated with the description **“Father of the Communal Electorate”**.

### Important qualification

Muslims could also enter councils through nomination or other routes for which they were eligible.

Do not interpret separate electorates as meaning that every Muslim councillor had to be elected through a Muslim constituency.

---

## 6. Weightage and Special-Interest Representation

### A. Weightage

**Weightage** means representation greater than a community’s share of the population.

| Concept | Main question |
|---|---|
| **Separate electorate** | Who elects the representative? |
| **Weightage** | How much representation is allotted? |

The reform arrangements accorded Muslims preferential representation beyond a strictly population-based allocation in relevant provinces and constituencies.

The arrangements were not identical everywhere.

### B. Special-interest representation

Representation was also provided for institutions and interests such as:

- Presidency corporations.
- Chambers of commerce.
- Universities.
- Zamindars and landholders.

### Important distinction

Representation of landholders or commercial bodies was based on **economic or institutional interests**.

Muslim separate electorates were based on **religious community**.

These should not be treated as the same form of representation.

---

## 7. Expanded Legislative Powers

Members of central and provincial legislative councils obtained wider opportunities to discuss government policy.

They could, subject to the prescribed rules:

1. Ask questions.
2. Ask **supplementary questions**.
3. Discuss the annual financial statement.
4. Move **resolutions on permitted budget matters**.
5. Move resolutions on matters of **general public interest**.

### Supplementary question

A follow-up question arising from the answer to an earlier question.

- **1892:** No right to ask supplementary questions.
- **1909:** Supplementary questions permitted, subject to restrictions.

### Resolution

A formal proposal expressing the council’s opinion or recommendation.

### Budget powers: the essential distinction

Members could move and vote on permitted budget-related resolutions.

However:

- Resolutions did not generally bind the executive.
- Members did not acquire full control over government expenditure.
- They could not compel the government to resign by rejecting its financial policy.

> **Voting on a budget-related resolution was not equivalent to parliamentary control over the budget.**

---

## 8. Indians in Executive Councils

### Satyendra Prasanna Sinha

**Satyendra Prasanna Sinha — S. P. Sinha — became the first Indian member of the Viceroy’s Executive Council in 1909.**

He was appointed as the **Law Member**.

### Do not confuse the two developments

| Development | Year |
|---|---|
| Lord Canning nominated Indians to the expanded legislative council | **1862** |
| S. P. Sinha joined the Viceroy’s Executive Council | **1909** |

The first concerned **participation in law-making**; the second concerned membership of the **executive government**.

### Limit of the change

Sinha’s appointment was an important development in Indian participation, but it did not establish responsible government.

Executive members remained appointed officials, not ministers dependent on the confidence of an elected Indian legislature.

---

## 9. Continuing Limitations

The reforms expanded representation and discussion but retained colonial executive supremacy.

They did not introduce:

- Universal adult franchise.
- An elected majority at the Centre.
- Responsible parliamentary government.
- Full legislative control over the budget.
- Provincial dyarchy.
- Provincial autonomy.

### Meaning of responsible government

The executive must retain the confidence of the representative legislature to remain in office.

This relationship was **not established in 1909**.

### Avoid an overstatement

The councils did more than merely criticise: they participated in legislation and exercised deliberative powers.

Their principal limitation was that they **could not control or remove the executive**.

---

## 10. Additional Prelims Facts

### A. Provincial membership ceilings

The Act’s schedule prescribed the following maximum numbers of nominated and elected members:

| Provincial legislative councils | Maximum under the schedule |
|---|---|
| Madras, Bombay, Bengal, United Provinces, Eastern Bengal and Assam | **50 each** |
| Punjab and Burma | **30 each** |

These are statutory ceilings, not a statement that every council had an identical total membership.

### B. Provincial executive councils

The Act:

- Permitted enlargement of the executive councils of **Bombay and Madras** to a maximum of **four ordinary members**.
- Provided machinery for creating executive councils in provinces under Lieutenant-Governors, subject to prescribed approvals.

### C. Electoral qualifications

Textbooks highlight more favourable qualifications for Muslim voters in certain respects, including lower income qualifications than corresponding non-Muslim requirements.

Treat this as a feature of the detailed electoral regulations—not as a single uniform qualification applicable throughout India.

### D. Act versus regulations

The Act supplied the constitutional framework. Rules and regulations implemented details such as:

- Electoral constituencies.
- Qualifications for voters and candidates.
- Separate-electorate arrangements.
- Procedures for questions and resolutions.

---

## 11. Comparison: Acts of 1861, 1892 and 1909

| Feature | 1861 | 1892 | 1909 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central additional membership | **6–12** | **10–16** | Maximum **60** |
| Selection | Nomination | Recommendations followed by nomination for some seats | Nomination and explicitly recognised election |
| Indian legislative participation | Began under this framework | Expanded | Further expanded |
| General budget discussion | Not provided | Permitted | Expanded, with budget-related resolutions |
| Supplementary questions | Not provided | Not permitted | Permitted subject to restrictions |
| Muslim separate electorates | No | No | **Yes** |
| Responsible government | No | No | No |

---

## 12. Final Revision Table

| Topic | Remember |
|---|---|
| Names | **Morley: Secretary of State; Minto: Viceroy** |
| Central membership | Maximum **60 additional members** |
| Central majority | **Official majority** |
| Provincial majority | **Non-official majority**, not necessarily elected majority |
| Electoral innovation | **Muslim separate electorates** |
| Weightage | Representation exceeding population share |
| Legislative powers | Supplementary questions and resolutions |
| First Indian executive member | **S. P. Sinha — Law Member — 1909** |
| Provincial dyarchy | **1919 Act**, not 1909 |
| Provincial autonomy | **1935 Act**, implemented in 1937 |

# Government of India Act, 1919 : Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms

## 1. Identity and Historical Background

The **Government of India Act, 1919**, implemented through rules and subsequent administrative arrangements, is associated with the **Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms**.

| Person | Office |
|---|---|
| **Edwin Montagu** | Secretary of State for India |
| **Lord Chelmsford** | Viceroy of India |

### Important dates

| Date/Year | Development |
|---|---|
| **20 August 1917** | Montagu Declaration |
| **1918** | Montagu–Chelmsford Report |
| **23 December 1919** | Royal assent to the Act |
| **1921** | New constitutional arrangements became operational |

### Background to the reforms

Important developments included:

- Dissatisfaction with the limited Morley–Minto Reforms.
- India’s contribution to the First World War.
- Growing demands for self-government.
- The Home Rule movement.
- The Congress–League Lucknow Pact of 1916.

---

## 2. Montagu Declaration, 1917

On **20 August 1917**, the British government announced a policy of:

- Increasing Indian participation in administration.
- Gradually developing self-governing institutions.
- Progressively realising **responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire**.

### What did this mean?

It recognised responsible government as a future objective.

However:

- It did not promise immediate self-government.
- It did not promise independence.
- The pace and manner of constitutional progress remained under British control.

### Meaning of responsible government

An executive is responsible to a representative legislature when it must retain that legislature’s confidence to remain in office.

> The Act introduced a limited element of responsible government in the provinces. It did not establish responsible government at the Centre.

---

## 3. The Basic Structure: Three Distinctions

| Question | Arrangement under the 1919 reforms |
|---|---|
| How were responsibilities divided territorially? | **Central and provincial subjects** |
| How were provincial subjects administered? | **Reserved and transferred subjects** |
| How was the central legislature organised? | **Two Houses: Council of State and Legislative Assembly** |

### Do not confuse

- **Devolution:** Allocation of functions between the Centre and provinces.
- **Dyarchy:** Division of provincial administration into reserved and transferred branches.
- **Bicameralism:** A legislature consisting of two Houses.

These were different features of the reforms.

---

## 4. Division into Central and Provincial Subjects

The reforms demarcated **central subjects** and **provincial subjects**.

### Illustrative classification

| Central subjects | Provincial subjects |
|---|---|
| Defence | Police |
| Foreign affairs | Administration of justice |
| Railways | Land revenue |
| Posts and telegraphs | Agriculture |
| Currency | Education and public health |

### Constitutional significance

The arrangement relaxed central control over some provincial functions and expanded provincial administrative responsibilities.

### Important limitation

It did **not establish a federation**.

The constitutional structure remained substantially **unitary**, with overriding authority retained by the central government and British authorities.

> Division of subjects did not itself make the provinces constitutionally autonomous.

---

## 5. Provincial Dyarchy: The Most Important Provision

The Act introduced **dyarchy in the Governor’s provinces**.

**Dyarchy means dual rule.**

Provincial subjects were divided into:

1. **Reserved subjects**
2. **Transferred subjects**

### Administration and responsibility

| Feature | Reserved subjects | Transferred subjects |
|---|---|---|
| Administered by | **Governor with his Executive Council** | **Governor acting with ministers** |
| Responsibility to provincial legislature | Executive councillors were **not responsible** to it | Ministers were responsible to it, subject to the Governor’s overriding powers |
| General character | Core administrative, coercive and financial functions | Mainly welfare and development functions |
| Examples | Police, justice, finance, land revenue, irrigation | Education, public health, agriculture, local self-government |

### Important subject pairs

| Transferred | Reserved |
|---|---|
| **Agriculture** | **Irrigation** |
| **Education and public health** | **Finance** |
| **Local self-government** | **Police and administration of justice** |

These divisions could create practical difficulties: a minister responsible for agriculture did not control irrigation or the main financial machinery.

### Critical trap

**Defence and foreign affairs were central subjects.**

They were not “reserved provincial subjects” merely because they remained under British control.

---

## 6. Position of Provincial Ministers and the Governor

### Provincial ministers

- Appointed by the **Governor**.
- Administered transferred subjects.
- Had to be, or become within **six months**, elected members of the provincial legislature.
- Held office during the Governor’s pleasure.
- Were expected to be answerable to the provincial legislature for their departments.

### Governor’s powers

The Governor presided over both branches of provincial government.

He could:

- Act with his Executive Council on reserved subjects.
- Act with ministers on transferred subjects.
- Depart from ministerial advice where the statutory conditions permitted.
- Exercise important powers over legislation and finance.

### Why was responsible government incomplete?

Ministers faced two sources of pressure:

- The legislature, to which they were politically answerable.
- The Governor, who appointed them and possessed overriding powers.

There was no unified cabinet collectively controlling the whole provincial administration.

### Why did dyarchy encounter difficulties?

- Closely connected departments were divided between the two branches.
- Ministers lacked control over reserved finance.
- Executive councillors were not accountable to the legislature.
- Governors retained substantial discretion.
- Responsibility was divided without a corresponding unified control over administration.

---

## 7. Where Was Dyarchy Introduced?

It was initially introduced in **eight Governor’s provinces**:

1. Bengal
2. Bombay
3. Madras
4. United Provinces
5. Punjab
6. Bihar and Orissa
7. Central Provinces
8. Assam

### Essential distinction

> **1919: Dyarchy in the provinces—not at the Centre.**

### Comparison with 1935

| Act | Dyarchy |
|---|---|
| **Government of India Act, 1919** | Introduced in the provinces |
| **Government of India Act, 1935** | Abolished provincial dyarchy and provided for dyarchy at the Centre |
| Implementation of central dyarchy under 1935 | **Never came into operation**, because the proposed federation was not established |

---

## 8. Provincial Legislative Councils

The provincial legislatures remained **unicameral**.

Each Governor’s province had a **Legislative Council**.

### Composition

- At least **70% of members were to be elected**.
- Not more than **20% were to be officials**.
- The normal term was **three years**, subject to dissolution or permitted extension.

### Why this matters

Under the 1909 reforms, a provincial **non-official majority** did not necessarily mean an elected majority.

Under the 1919 arrangements, provincial councils had a substantial **elected majority**.

However, the Governor’s powers and the reserved branch still limited legislative control.

---

## 9. Bicameralism at the Centre

The Act introduced a **bicameral central legislature**.

The earlier single legislative council was replaced by:

1. **Council of State — Upper House**
2. **Legislative Assembly — Lower House**

### Composition under the arrangements brought into operation

| Feature | Council of State | Legislative Assembly |
|---|---|---|
| Position | Upper House | Lower House |
| Membership | **60** | **145** |
| Elected members | **34** | **104** |
| Nominated members | **26** | **41** |
| Normal term | **5 years** | **3 years** |

Both Houses had an **elected majority**.

### Numerical qualification

The Act initially specified **140 members** for the Legislative Assembly but permitted the number to be increased through rules. The commonly used figure **145** refers to the constituted arrangement.

### Important distinctions

- **Bicameralism was introduced at the Centre.**
- Provincial legislatures under this scheme remained unicameral.
- An elected majority in the central legislature did not make the central executive responsible to it.
- The historical **Council of State** should not be treated as identical to today’s Rajya Sabha.

---

## 10. Direct Elections and Restricted Franchise

The reforms expanded direct elections to the legislatures.

### What is a direct election?

Eligible voters choose the legislative representative themselves rather than selecting another body that subsequently elects the representative.

### What remained restricted?

Voting rights depended on qualifications involving:

- Property.
- Tax payment.
- Education or other prescribed criteria.

There was **no universal adult franchise**.

### Wording to remember

Textbooks commonly associate the 1919 Act with the introduction of direct elections.

The useful distinction is that the reforms established a substantially expanded system of direct elections, rather than giving every adult the vote.

---

## 11. Extension of Separate Electorates

The reforms continued Muslim separate electorates and extended separate electoral arrangements to groups including:

- **Sikhs**
- **Indian Christians**
- **Anglo-Indians**
- **Europeans**

### Comparison

| Reform | Electoral development |
|---|---|
| **1909** | Muslim separate electorates introduced |
| **1919** | Separate electoral arrangements extended to additional communities/groups |

Representation of commercial, landed and other special interests also continued.

> The 1919 reforms extended separate representation; they did not originate Muslim separate electorates.

---

## 12. Central Executive and Indian Participation

Under the reform arrangements, **three of the six ordinary members of the Viceroy’s Executive Council were Indians**, excluding the Commander-in-Chief from this count.

### Important limitation

Indian membership did not establish a parliamentary executive.

- Executive members were appointed.
- The central executive did not depend on the confidence of the Legislative Assembly.
- The legislature could not replace the government through a vote of no confidence.

### Earlier development

**S. P. Sinha** had already become the first Indian member of the Viceroy’s Executive Council in **1909**.

Therefore, 1919 expanded Indian participation; it did not begin it.

---

## 13. Financial Changes and Legislative Control

### A. Separation of budgets

The reforms separated **provincial budgets from the central budget** and allocated revenues between the two levels.

Provincial legislatures obtained greater opportunities to consider expenditure and vote on grants, subject to statutory restrictions.

### B. Central financial powers

The **Legislative Assembly** obtained the power to vote on demands for grants relating to votable expenditure.

However:

- Significant expenditure was not submitted to its vote.
- The Governor-General could restore expenditure considered necessary under his statutory powers.
- The government was not required to resign because it lost a financial vote.

### Important distinction

| Feature | Position |
|---|---|
| Power to discuss finances | Expanded |
| Power to vote on specified grants | Introduced under the new arrangements |
| Complete control over expenditure | Not granted |
| Central executive responsibility to the legislature | Not established |

---

## 14. Governor-General’s Overriding Powers

The Governor-General retained strong authority over central legislation and finance.

His powers included:

- Withholding assent to legislation.
- Reserving bills for the Crown’s decision.
- Securing enactment of legislation considered essential under the statutory safeguards, despite legislative opposition.
- Exercising overriding financial powers.
- Issuing ordinances under the applicable emergency provisions.

### Meaning for Prelims

The central legislature became more representative, but it was neither sovereign nor capable of controlling the executive in the manner of a parliamentary legislature.

---

## 15. Changes in Administration in Britain

### A. High Commissioner for India

The Act provided for a **High Commissioner for India in the United Kingdom**, based in London.

Certain functions previously exercised by the Secretary of State were transferred or delegated to this office, particularly commercial and agency work such as purchasing stores and handling contracts.

### Do not confuse the offices

| Office | Main position |
|---|---|
| Secretary of State for India | British Cabinet minister exercising political supervision over Indian administration |
| High Commissioner for India | Handled specified functions in Britain on behalf of Indian governments |
| Viceroy and Governor-General | Head of the government in India |

The High Commissioner did not replace the Secretary of State.

### B. Secretary of State’s salary

The Secretary of State’s salary was shifted from **Indian revenues to the British Exchequer**.

---

## 16. Public Service Commission

The Act provided for the establishment of a **Public Service Commission**.

The first Public Service Commission in India was established on:

**1 October 1926**

### Important sequence

| Year | Development |
|---|---|
| **1919** | Statutory provision for a Public Service Commission |
| **1926** | First Public Service Commission established |
| **1935 Act** | Provided for a Federal Public Service Commission and Provincial Public Service Commissions |
| **1950** | UPSC acquired its position under the Constitution of independent India |

> Do not confuse the year of the enabling provision with the year of establishment.

---

## 17. Statutory Review and Simon Commission

The Act provided for a commission to examine the working of the constitutional system **after ten years from the passing of the Act**.

### Important correction

The statutory wording referred to ten years after **passing in 1919**, not ten years after implementation in 1921.

### Simon Commission

The review commission was appointed ahead of the intended ten-year point:

- **Appointed:** 1927
- **Chairman:** Sir John Simon
- **Members:** Seven; all British
- **Arrived in India:** 1928

Its lack of Indian members led to widespread opposition and boycott.

### Prelims distinction

**1927: Appointment**  
**1928: Arrival in India**

---

## 18. Comparison: Reforms of 1909 and 1919

| Feature | 1909 | 1919 |
|---|---|---|
| Associated names | Morley–Minto | Montagu–Chelmsford |
| Central legislature | Unicameral | Bicameral |
| Central legislative majority | Official majority | Elected majority in both Houses |
| Provincial administration | No dyarchy | Dyarchy |
| Provincial legislatures | Non-official majorities | Substantial elected majorities |
| Separate electorates | Introduced for Muslims | Extended to additional groups |
| Financial role | Discussion and resolutions | Voting on specified grants, with safeguards |
| Responsible government at Centre | No | No |
| Universal adult franchise | No | No |

---

## 19. Final Prelims Revision

| Topic | Remember |
|---|---|
| Declaration | **20 August 1917** |
| Reform names | **Montagu–Chelmsford** |
| Act and implementation | **1919; operational arrangements from 1921** |
| Territorial division | Central and provincial subjects |
| Provincial division | Reserved and transferred subjects |
| Reserved examples | Police, justice, finance, land revenue, irrigation |
| Transferred examples | Education, health, agriculture, local self-government |
| Dyarchy | Provinces only |
| Bicameralism | Centre only |
| Central Houses | Council of State and Legislative Assembly |
| Franchise | Restricted |
| PSC | Provision in 1919; established in 1926 |
| Review | Ten years after passing |
| Simon Commission | Appointed 1927; arrived 1928 |

# Simon Commission : Indian Statutory Commission, 1927

## 1. Identity at a Glance

| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Official name | **Indian Statutory Commission** |
| Popular name | **Simon Commission** |
| Appointment announced | **8 November 1927** |
| Chairman | **Sir John Simon** |
| Composition | **Seven British members; no Indian member** |
| First arrival in India | **3 February 1928, Bombay** |
| Report | **1930** |
| Legal background | Review provision under the **Government of India Act, 1919** |
| Principal task | Examine the working of the constitutional system and recommend further reforms |

### Why “statutory”?

A **statutory commission** is a commission whose appointment is provided for by law.

The Simon Commission arose from the review provision associated with the Government of India Act, 1919.

---

## 2. Background: The Government of India Act, 1919

The Act of 1919 had introduced:

- **Dyarchy in the provinces**.
- A **bicameral central legislature**.
- Expanded elections under a restricted franchise.
- A limited element of responsible government in transferred provincial departments.

### Meaning of provincial dyarchy

| Branch | Administration |
|---|---|
| **Reserved subjects** | Governor and his Executive Council |
| **Transferred subjects** | Governor acting with ministers responsible to the provincial legislature, subject to his overriding powers |

The arrangement suffered from divided authority, financial constraints and difficulties in coordinating related departments.

### Statutory review

The Act provided for a commission after **ten years from its passing** to examine the working of the constitutional system.

| Event | Year |
|---|---|
| Act passed | **1919** |
| Ten-year review point | **1929** |
| Commission appointed | **1927** |

> The Commission was appointed approximately **two years ahead of schedule**.

**Prelims distinction:** Count from the Act’s passing in **1919**, not the implementation of the new arrangements in **1921**.

---

## 3. Why Was the Commission Appointed Early?

### Administrative background

The British government considered it necessary to review the working of the 1919 reforms and the difficulties surrounding dyarchy.

### Political calculation in Britain

The Conservative government wanted to influence the constitutional reform process before a possible electoral defeat brought the Labour Party to power.

This was a political consideration behind early appointment, not a statutory requirement.

### Important officeholders

| Person | Position at the time of appointment |
|---|---|
| **Stanley Baldwin** | British Prime Minister |
| **Lord Birkenhead** | Secretary of State for India |
| **Lord Irwin** | Viceroy of India |
| **Sir John Simon** | Commission chairman |

---

## 4. Composition and Mandate

### Composition

- Seven members.
- All were British parliamentarians.
- Different British political parties were represented.
- **No Indian was included.**

### Clement Attlee

**Clement Attlee** was a member.

He later became the British Prime Minister during the transfer of power to India in **1947**.

### What was the Commission to examine?

Broadly:

- The working of the constitutional arrangements introduced under the 1919 Act.
- The development of representative institutions.
- Whether responsible government should be extended, modified or restricted.
- The direction of further constitutional reform.

### Important distinction

The Commission was not appointed to investigate a specific incident of colonial violence.

| Body | Principal purpose |
|---|---|
| **Hunter Committee** | Inquiry into the Punjab disturbances of 1919, including the Jallianwala Bagh massacre |
| **Simon Commission** | Constitutional review associated with the Government of India Act, 1919 |

---

## 5. Why Was It Boycotted?

The immediate and principal reason was the **exclusion of Indians from its membership**.

Indian nationalists objected that foreigners would judge India’s fitness for self-government without including Indians in the decision-making body.

They regarded this as:

- An insult to Indian political opinion.
- A denial of the principle of self-determination.
- Evidence that Britain intended to retain control over India’s constitutional future.

### Important chronological distinction

The boycott began before the Commission published its recommendations.

> The original boycott was primarily against the Commission’s **composition**, not against the contents of its **1930 report**.

---

## 6. Congress Decision and Other Political Responses

### Congress decision

The Congress decided to boycott the Commission at its:

- **Madras Session, December 1927**
- **President: Dr M. A. Ansari**

The decision envisaged boycott at every stage and in every form.

### Political responses

| Organisation or group | Broad position |
|---|---|
| Indian National Congress | Boycott |
| Indian Liberals | Boycott |
| Hindu Mahasabha | Boycott |
| Muslim League faction led by **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** | Boycott |
| Muslim League faction led by **Sir Muhammad Shafi** | Cooperation |
| Justice Party in Madras | Cooperation |
| Punjab Unionists | Did not join the boycott |

### Important correction

The claim that **“all Indian parties boycotted the Simon Commission”** is incorrect.

The opposition was widespread, but some groups cooperated.

### Muslim League distinction

The League was divided over its response:

- **Jinnah faction:** Opposed the Commission.
- **Shafi faction:** Cooperated.

Do not attribute one uniform position to the entire Muslim League.

---

## 7. Arrival and Public Protests

The Commission first arrived at **Bombay on 3 February 1928**.

The protests included:

- Hartals.
- Black-flag demonstrations.
- Public meetings.
- Processions.
- The slogan **“Simon Go Back”**.

### Dates to distinguish

| Event | Date/Year |
|---|---|
| Appointment announced | **8 November 1927** |
| First arrival in India | **3 February 1928** |
| Report | **1930** |

---

## 8. Police Repression and Lala Lajpat Rai

### Important protest centres

| Place | Leaders associated with protests and police assault |
|---|---|
| **Lahore** | Lala Lajpat Rai |
| **Lucknow** | Jawaharlal Nehru and Govind Ballabh Pant |

### Lahore events

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **30 October 1928** | Lala Lajpat Rai led an anti-Simon demonstration in Lahore and was injured in a police lathi-charge |
| **17 November 1928** | Lala Lajpat Rai died following the injuries |

### Related revolutionary action

Revolutionaries sought to avenge Lajpat Rai’s death.

- **Intended target:** Police officer **James A. Scott**.
- **Officer killed:** **J. P. Saunders**, in a case of mistaken identity.
- **Date:** **17 December 1928**.
- The action was associated with **Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and their revolutionary associates**.

### Prelims traps

- Lajpat Rai did not die on the day of the lathi-charge.
- The intended target was Scott; the officer killed was Saunders.
- The killing of Saunders was separate from the Central Legislative Assembly bombing of **1929**.

---

## 9. Youth Participation and Nationalist Mobilisation

The anti-Simon agitation:

- Provided an issue around which widespread political mobilisation could occur.
- Increased participation by students and young activists.
- Strengthened the prominence of **Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose**.
- Encouraged discussion of complete independence and socialist ideas.
- Reinforced the demand that Indians should formulate their own constitutional proposals.

### Chronological caution

Organisations such as the **Naujawan Bharat Sabha** and **Hindustani Seva Dal** already existed before the anti-Simon agitation.

Do not state that they were originally founded because of the Commission.

### Madras 1927 versus Lahore 1929

| Congress session | Important development |
|---|---|
| **Madras, 1927 — M. A. Ansari** | Simon Commission boycott decision; an independence resolution moved by Jawaharlal Nehru was passed |
| **Lahore, 1929 — Jawaharlal Nehru** | Decisive adoption of **Purna Swaraj** as the Congress programme |

**26 January 1930** was subsequently observed as Independence Day.

---

## 10. Simon Commission Report, 1930

The Commission’s main report consisted of two volumes:

1. **Survey**
2. **Recommendations**

### Date clarification

For Prelims revision, remember **1930**.

Although some textbooks use “May 1930”, the two principal volumes were publicly issued in **June 1930**, with the recommendations volume appearing on **24 June**.

### Nature of the report

It contained recommendations.

It did not itself enact a constitution or immediately change the system of government.

---

## 11. Principal Recommendations

### A. Abolish provincial dyarchy

The Commission recommended ending the division between reserved and transferred provincial subjects.

This would permit a more unified provincial administration.

> **1919 Act introduced provincial dyarchy; Simon Commission recommended its abolition.**

### B. Extend responsible government in the provinces

The report favoured:

- Greater provincial autonomy.
- Extension of responsible provincial government.
- Enlargement of provincial legislative councils.

### C. Retain Governors’ safeguards

Governors were to retain special powers, particularly concerning:

- Internal security and public order.
- Protection of minority interests.
- Specified administrative safeguards.

Thus, greater provincial responsibility did not mean unrestricted provincial sovereignty.

### D. No immediate parliamentary responsibility at the Centre

The Commission did not recommend immediately making the central executive responsible to the Indian legislature.

Substantial authority was to remain with the Governor-General and the central executive.

### E. Federal development as a future objective

The Commission accepted a federal direction involving:

- British Indian provinces.
- Indian princely states.

However, it did not regard an immediate All-India Federation as practicable.

It proposed a **Consultative Council of Greater India** to bring representatives of British India and princely states into consultation.

### F. Continue communal electorates

The Commission recommended continuing communal electorates.

It did not recommend their immediate replacement by joint electorates throughout India.

### G. Restricted franchise

The report did not accept universal adult franchise.

Wider representation was to operate within a restricted electoral system.

### H. Separate Burma from India

The Commission recommended the separation of **Burma from British India**.

Burma was subsequently separated in **1937** under the later constitutional arrangements.

# Delhi Proposals, 1927 : Delhi Muslim Proposals

## 1. Identity at a Glance

| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Also known as | **Delhi Muslim Proposals** |
| Date | **20 March 1927** |
| Place | **Delhi** |
| Principal leader | **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** |
| Participants | A meeting of prominent Muslim leaders |
| Main objective | Reach a constitutional agreement on Muslim representation and electoral safeguards |
| Central offer | Conditional acceptance of **joint electorates** in place of separate electorates |

### Important date correction

The proposals originated in **March 1927**, not December 1927.

Their consideration by political organisations later in the year should not be confused with their formulation.

---

## 2. Historical Background

### A. Separate electorates

The **Morley–Minto Reforms of 1909** introduced separate electorates for Muslims.

Under this arrangement, qualified Muslim voters elected representatives for Muslim separate-electorate seats.

The **1919 reforms** continued and extended separate electoral arrangements.

### B. Search for a constitutional agreement

Indian political leaders were attempting to reach an agreed position on constitutional reform.

A major disagreement concerned:

- Joint versus separate electorates.
- Muslim representation at the Centre.
- Representation in Muslim-majority provinces.
- Constitutional reforms in frontier territories.

The Delhi Proposals attempted to resolve these questions through a negotiated package.

### Important chronological distinction

The proposals preceded the appointment of the Simon Commission:

| Event | Date |
|---|---|
| Delhi Proposals | **20 March 1927** |
| Simon Commission appointment announced | **8 November 1927** |

> The Delhi Proposals were not formulated in response to the Simon Commission’s appointment or its report.

---

## 3. The Central Bargain

Jinnah and the participating Muslim leaders offered to accept **joint electorates**, provided specified constitutional safeguards were accepted.

### Meaning

The offer was not:

> “Separate electorates are abandoned unconditionally.”

It was:

> “Joint electorates can be accepted if the accompanying safeguards concerning representation and provincial reform are accepted.”

The proposals were a negotiating formula, not a law that immediately altered electoral arrangements.

---

## 4. Four Principal Conditions

### 1. Separation of Sindh from Bombay

**Sindh should be separated from the Bombay Presidency and constituted as a separate province.**

#### Significance

Sindh had a Muslim-majority population but formed part of the larger Bombay Presidency.

A separate province would give its population a distinct provincial political framework.

#### Prelims distinction

- **Demand:** Separation proposed in 1927.
- **Actual separation:** **1936**, under the later constitutional arrangements.

The Delhi Proposals did not themselves create the province.

---

### 2. Constitutional Reforms in NWFP and Baluchistan

The **North-West Frontier Province and Baluchistan** should receive constitutional reforms on the same footing as other provinces.

#### Meaning

The demand concerned extending representative institutions and constitutional reforms to these territories.

#### Important wording

Do not describe this simply as the creation of “three entirely new provinces”.

The demands were:

- **Separate Sindh from Bombay.**
- **Extend reforms to NWFP and Baluchistan.**

NWFP already existed as an administrative province.

---

### 3. Population-Based Representation in Punjab and Bengal

In **Punjab and Bengal**, representation should be in accordance with population.

#### Significance

Muslims formed a majority in these provinces.

The demand sought to ensure that their legislative representation reflected their population strength.

#### Important distinction

This was a demand for **population-proportionate representation in Punjab and Bengal**.

It was not a demand for one-third representation in these provinces.

---

### 4. At Least One-Third Muslim Representation at the Centre

Muslims should receive **not less than one-third representation in the Central Legislature**.

#### Meaning

The proposed central share was a specified minimum.

It should not be confused with the population-based formula proposed for Punjab and Bengal.

### Comparison

| Level | Proposed basis of Muslim representation |
|---|---|
| **Central Legislature** | **At least one-third** |
| **Punjab and Bengal legislatures** | **According to population** |

---

## 5. Joint Electorates and Reserved Representation

### Separate electorate

Only eligible voters of the specified community elect the representative for that community’s seat.

### Joint electorate with reservation

All eligible voters in the constituency participate, but the specified seat is reserved for a candidate belonging to the protected community.

| Feature | Separate electorate | Joint electorate with a reserved seat |
|---|---|---|
| Candidate for the specified seat | Belongs to the specified community | Belongs to the specified community |
| Who votes? | Eligible voters of that community | All eligible voters in the constituency |

### Delhi Proposals: key point

The participating leaders were prepared to give up separate electorates in exchange for an agreed system of representation and safeguards.

> **Joint electorates did not mean abandoning safeguards for Muslim representation.**

### Reciprocal minority protection

The proposals also envisaged reciprocal safeguards for Hindu minorities in Muslim-majority provinces, corresponding to safeguards for Muslim minorities elsewhere.

---

## 6. Political Response

### Congress

Congress responded favourably to the possibility of a settlement based on joint electorates and safeguards.

The proposals formed an important part of the negotiations considered during 1927, including at the **Madras Congress Session in December 1927**.

### Differences among Muslim leaders

Acceptance was not universal.

- **Jinnah** supported the conditional joint-electorate formula.
- Leaders associated with **Muhammad Shafi** opposed abandoning separate electorates.

### Hindu Mahasabha objections

The Hindu Mahasabha opposed important elements of the proposed settlement, including demands concerning Muslim-majority provinces and communal representation.

### Outcome

The proposals opened an opportunity for agreement but did not produce a durable, universally accepted constitutional settlement.

> Do not state that all Muslim leaders permanently abandoned separate electorates in 1927.

---

## 7. Connection with the Nehru Report and Fourteen Points

### A. Nehru Report, 1928

The Nehru Report:

- Favoured joint electorates.
- Proposed minority safeguards.
- Did not incorporate the entire Delhi package in the form demanded by Jinnah and his supporters.

Important disagreements persisted over:

- Muslim representation at the Centre.
- Representation in Punjab and Bengal.
- The distribution of residuary powers.

### B. Jinnah’s amendments, December 1928

During discussions on the Nehru Report, Jinnah sought:

1. **One-third Muslim representation at the Centre.**
2. Population-proportionate Muslim representation in Punjab and Bengal until adult suffrage was established.
3. **Residuary powers for the provinces.**

These amendments were not accepted.

### C. Jinnah’s Fourteen Points, 1929

The Fourteen Points reaffirmed **separate electorates**, alongside other safeguards.

### Essential comparison

| Document | Electoral position |
|---|---|
| **Delhi Proposals, 1927** | Conditional willingness to accept joint electorates |
| **Nehru Report, 1928** | Joint electorates with specified minority safeguards |
| **Jinnah’s Fourteen Points, 1929** | Separate electorates retained as a demand |

### Do not mix the documents

**Residuary powers for provinces** is particularly associated with Jinnah’s amendments and Fourteen Points.

It is not one of the four principal conditions of the Delhi Proposals.

---

## 8. Significance

The Delhi Proposals were important because they:

- Offered a possible compromise over separate electorates.
- Linked electoral arrangements with provincial reform.
- Distinguished Muslim representation at the Centre from representation in Muslim-majority provinces.
- Influenced the constitutional negotiations preceding the Nehru Report.
- Demonstrated that political positions on electorates were conditional and contested.

### What they were not

They were not:

- An Act of the British Parliament.
- A British constitutional award.
- An immediate abolition of separate electorates.
- A demand for the creation of Pakistan.
- The same document as Jinnah’s Fourteen Points.

**Conditional joint electorates in exchange for constitutional safeguards.**

### Four conditions

1. **Sindh:** Separate from Bombay.
2. **NWFP and Baluchistan:** Reforms on the same footing as other provinces.
3. **Punjab and Bengal:** Representation according to population.
4. **Centre:** At least one-third Muslim representation.

### Common traps

| Statement | Correct position |

# Nehru Report, 1928

## 1. At a Glance

| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Year | 1928 |
| Committee appointed by | All Parties Conference |
| Chairman | Motilal Nehru |
| Secretary | Jawaharlal Nehru |
| Report finalised | August 1928 |
| Constitutional status proposed | Dominion status |
| Government proposed | Responsible government at the Centre and in the provinces |
| Residuary powers | With the Centre |
| Electoral system | Joint electorates with reservation of seats for specified minorities |
| Franchise | Universal adult suffrage |
| Nature | An Indian proposal for a constitutional framework; not an enacted law |

> **Remember: Motilal Nehru chaired the committee—not Jawaharlal Nehru.**

---

## 2. Historical Background

### A. Dissatisfaction with the Government of India Act, 1919

The 1919 Act had introduced dyarchy in the provinces but had not established fully responsible government.

Indian political leaders demanded further constitutional changes.

### B. Appointment of the Simon Commission

The British government appointed the **Simon Commission in November 1927** to review the constitutional arrangements under the 1919 Act.

- All seven members were British.
- The exclusion of Indians provoked widespread opposition.
- Indian leaders objected to a constitutional review conducted without Indian membership.

### C. Lord Birkenhead’s challenge

**Lord Birkenhead**, the Secretary of State for India, challenged Indian leaders to produce a constitutional scheme acceptable to the major political groups.

The Nehru Report emerged as an Indian response to this challenge.

> **Prelims distinction:** The Nehru Committee was appointed by the All Parties Conference, not by the British government or the Simon Commission.

---

## 3. Formation of the Nehru Committee

- An **All Parties Conference met in February 1928** to discuss constitutional reform.
- At its **Bombay meeting in May 1928**, a committee was appointed under **Motilal Nehru** to prepare a constitutional framework.
- **Jawaharlal Nehru served as secretary.**
- The report was finalised in **August 1928**.

Other members included:

- Tej Bahadur Sapru
- Subhas Chandra Bose
- M. S. Aney
- Mangal Singh
- Ali Imam
- Shuaib Qureshi
- G. R. Pradhan

### Significance

It was a major collective Indian effort to prepare a comprehensive constitutional framework.

**Careful wording:** It is commonly described as the first major Indian attempt to draft a constitutional framework. Earlier Indian constitutional proposals also existed.

---

## 4. Dominion Status

The report recommended **Dominion status within the British Empire**, on the lines of self-governing dominions such as Canada and Australia.

### Meaning

India would have substantial self-government, with a parliamentary government, while retaining its constitutional connection with the British Crown.

### Important distinction

| Nehru Report, 1928 | Lahore Congress, 1929 |
|---|---|
| Dominion status | Purna Swaraj or complete independence |

> **The Nehru Report did not demand complete independence as its constitutional objective.**

---

## 5. Responsible Government at the Centre and in the Provinces

The report proposed responsible government at **both levels**.

### Meaning of responsible government

The executive would be accountable to the legislature and would require its support to remain in office.

### At the Centre

- A **Governor-General** would represent the Crown.
- He would act on the advice of the executive government.
- The executive would be **collectively responsible to the House of Representatives**.

### In the provinces

- Governors would act with ministerial governments responsible to the provincial legislatures.
- This meant replacing provincial dyarchy with responsible government.

> **Prelims distinction:** The report sought responsible government at both the Centre and the provincial level.

---

## 6. Distribution of Powers: A Strong Centre

The report proposed a distribution of powers between the Centre and the provinces, with a **strong Centre**.

### Residuary powers

**Residuary powers were to belong to the Centre.**

These are powers over subjects not specifically assigned in the constitutional distribution of subjects.

### Example

If a new subject arose and the Constitution had not assigned it to either level, the Centre would have authority over it.

### Scope of the scheme

- The immediate constitutional scheme principally concerned **British India**.
- It envisaged a future federal association with the **princely states**.

> **High-value distinction:** Nehru Report → residuary powers with the Centre. Jinnah’s later proposals → residuary powers with the provinces.

---

## 7. Parliamentary Structure

The report proposed a **bicameral Parliament** at the Centre.

| Feature | House of Representatives | Senate |
|---|---|---|
| Position | Lower House | Upper House |
| Membership | 500 | 200 |
| Election | Direct election on the basis of adult suffrage | Election by provincial councils |
| Normal tenure | 5 years | 7 years |

### Provincial legislatures

- Provincial legislative councils were proposed.
- Their normal tenure would be **five years**.

**Note:** These were proposed institutions; they were not established by the report.

---

## 8. Universal Adult Suffrage

The report recommended voting rights for **both men and women aged 21 years and above**, subject to legal disqualifications.

### Why this matters

The colonial electoral arrangements then in operation restricted voting rights through qualifications such as property, taxation and education.

The Nehru Report proposed a much broader franchise.

> **Prelims trap:** The proposed voting age was 21, not 18.

---

## 9. Fundamental Rights

The report included **19 clauses on fundamental rights**.

Important provisions included:

- Equality before the law.
- Equal civic rights.
- Equal rights for men and women.
- Freedom of conscience and religion.
- Freedom of expression.
- Freedom of peaceful assembly.
- Freedom to form associations and unions.
- Free elementary education.
- Protection of personal liberty.
- Equal access to public roads, wells and other public places.
- Protection against discrimination in public employment on grounds such as religion, caste or creed.

### Minority safeguards

It also proposed protection for the cultural, religious and educational interests of minorities.

> **Remember:** Fundamental rights, women’s equality and adult suffrage were important elements of the Nehru Report.

---

## 10. State and Religion

The report proposed that:

- There should be **no state religion**.
- The State should not favour or disadvantage people because of their religion.
- Religious freedom should be protected.

This is commonly summarised as the **separation of the State from religion**.

---

## 11. Joint Electorates and Minority Representation

### A. Rejection of separate electorates

The report rejected the system of separate communal electorates.

It favoured **joint electorates**.

### B. Reservation of seats

It nevertheless supported reservation of seats for specified minorities.

For Muslims, it proposed:

- Reservation at the Centre in proportion to population.
- Reservation in provinces where Muslims were a minority.
- No reservation for Muslims in the provincial legislatures of **Punjab and Bengal**, where they were a majority.
- The right to contest additional, unreserved seats.

Minority safeguards also included representation for non-Muslims in the North-West Frontier Province.

### C. Duration

The reservation arrangements were envisaged for **ten years**, with reconsideration thereafter.

### Understand the distinction

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Separate electorate | A community’s representatives are elected by voters belonging to that community |
| Joint electorate | Voters participate in a common electorate irrespective of community |
| Reserved seat | A seat is set aside for a specified community; this can operate within a joint electorate |

> **Rejecting separate electorates did not mean rejecting all reservation of seats.**

---

## 12. Provincial Reorganisation

The report supported:

### A. Linguistic provinces

Provinces should be reorganised with attention to **linguistic considerations**.

### B. Separation of Sindh from Bombay

It supported the separation of **Sindh from the Bombay Presidency**, with safeguards for its minorities.

### C. Reforms in the North-West Frontier Province

It supported bringing the **North-West Frontier Province** into line with the constitutional arrangements of other provinces.

The proposed arrangements also addressed **British Baluchistan**.

> **Prelims distinction:** Separation of Sindh from Bombay was a proposal. The Nehru Report itself did not create a new province.

---

## 13. Other Constitutional Proposals

The report also proposed:

- A **Supreme Court**.
- Constitutional protection for the judiciary.
- **Hindustani**, written in either the Nagari or Urdu script, as the language of the Commonwealth.
- Continued permission to use English.

These are supplementary facts; prioritise Dominion status, responsible government, adult suffrage, fundamental rights and communal representation.

---

## 14. Reactions to the Report

### A. Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose

They opposed the acceptance of **Dominion status** as the goal.

They favoured **complete independence**.

They were associated with the **Independence for India League**.

> **Important:** Jawaharlal Nehru’s role as committee secretary does not mean that he agreed with Dominion status as the final objective.

### B. Jinnah’s objections

Jinnah sought changes concerning Muslim representation and the distribution of powers.

At the **Calcutta All Parties Conference in December 1928**, he proposed:

1. **At least one-third Muslim representation in the central legislature.**
2. **Population-based reservation for Muslims in Punjab and Bengal if adult suffrage was not granted**, with reconsideration after ten years.
3. **Residuary powers for the provinces.**

These amendments were not accepted.

Jinnah subsequently formulated his **Fourteen Points in March 1929**.

### C. Other objections

The report did not secure agreement among all political and communal organisations.

Sections of Muslim, Hindu and Sikh political opinion objected to different aspects of its representation arrangements.

> **Avoid the statement: “The Nehru Report was unanimously accepted by all Indian political groups.”**

---

## 15. Congress Response and Subsequent Developments

### Calcutta Congress Session, December 1928

- Presided over by **Motilal Nehru**.
- Congress accepted Dominion status as an immediate demand.
- It gave the British government until the **end of 1929** to accept the proposed constitutional settlement.
- Failure would lead Congress to pursue complete independence and launch a struggle.

### Lahore Congress Session, December 1929

- Presided over by **Jawaharlal Nehru**.
- Congress adopted **Purna Swaraj** as its goal.

### 26 January 1930

Observed as **Independence Day** following the Lahore decision.

---

# Government of India Act, 1935

## 1. At a Glance

| Feature | Provision |
|---|---|
| Enacted by | British Parliament |
| Proposed constitutional structure | All-India Federation |
| Proposed federal units | British Indian provinces and acceding princely states |
| Division of legislative powers | Federal, Provincial and Concurrent Lists |
| Residuary legislative powers | Governor-General’s discretion |
| Provincial executive | Provincial autonomy replacing dyarchy |
| Proposed federal executive | Dyarchy |
| Provincial autonomy commenced | 1 April 1937 |
| All-India Federation | Never established |
| Dyarchy at the Centre | Never implemented |
| Federal Court | Established in 1937 |
| Franchise | Expanded but remained restricted |
| Fundamental rights | No comprehensive, enforceable bill of fundamental rights |

### The most important distinction

> **Provincial autonomy operated. The proposed All-India Federation and central dyarchy did not.**

---

## 2. Historical Background

The Act emerged from a series of constitutional discussions and investigations.

### A. Government of India Act, 1919

The 1919 Act had introduced:

- Dyarchy in the provinces.
- A bicameral legislature at the Centre.
- Limited electoral participation.

It did not establish responsible government at the Centre.

### B. Simon Commission

- Appointed in **1927**.
- Submitted its report in **1930**.
- Recommended the abolition of provincial dyarchy and further constitutional changes.

### C. Round Table Conferences

Three Round Table Conferences were held in London between **1930 and 1932**.

They discussed constitutional reforms, including federation and minority representation.

### D. White Paper and parliamentary scrutiny

- The British government issued a **White Paper on constitutional reforms in 1933**.
- A **Joint Select Committee of the British Parliament**, chaired by **Lord Linlithgow**, examined the proposals.
- These deliberations contributed to the Government of India Act, 1935.

### Important persons

| Person | Relevant association |
|---|---|
| Lord Willingdon | Viceroy when the Act was passed in 1935 |
| Lord Linlithgow | Chairman of the Joint Select Committee; Viceroy when provincial autonomy began in 1937 |

---

## 3. Length of the Act: Clarifying the Different Figures

For the **India-only Act**, the standard figure is:

> **321 sections and 10 schedules.**

The original combined legislation covering **India and Burma** contained **451 sections and 15 schedules**. It was subsequently separated into the Government of India Act and the Government of Burma Act.

**Prelims revision:** Associate **321 sections and 10 schedules** with the Government of India Act, 1935, in its India-only form.

---

## 4. Proposed All-India Federation

The Act provided for a federation consisting of:

1. **Governors’ Provinces**.
2. **Chief Commissioners’ Provinces**.
3. **Princely states that acceded to the federation**.

### British Indian provinces and princely states

| British Indian provinces | Princely states |
|---|---|
| Their inclusion was provided for under the federal scheme | Accession was voluntary |
| Constitutional arrangements were prescribed by the Act | Federal authority depended on the terms of accession |

### Instrument of Accession

A princely ruler willing to join had to execute an **Instrument of Accession**, accepted by the Crown.

It specified:

- The matters on which the federal legislature could legislate for that state.
- Any limitations on the exercise of federal authority in the state.

### Conditions for establishing the federation

The required accessions had to include states:

- Entitled to choose **at least 52 representatives in the Council of State**.
- Representing **at least half the aggregate population of the princely states** covered by the statutory calculation.

### Why did the federation not begin?

The necessary accessions from princely states were not secured.

Therefore:

- The All-India Federation was never established.
- Its proposed legislature and central dyarchy did not become operational.

> **Trap:** The Act did not automatically integrate all princely states into a functioning federation.

---

## 5. Three Legislative Lists

The Act distributed legislative subjects through three lists in its **Seventh Schedule**.

| List | Number of entries | Legislative authority |
|---|---:|---|
| Federal List | 59 | Federal legislature |
| Provincial List | 54 | Provincial legislatures |
| Concurrent List | 36 | Both levels |

### Examples

| Federal subjects | Provincial subjects | Concurrent subjects |
|---|---|---|
| Defence | Public order | Criminal law |
| External affairs | Police | Criminal procedure |
| Currency | Public health | Marriage and divorce |
| Posts and telegraphs | Agriculture | Certain labour and welfare matters |

### Residuary powers

Residuary subjects are matters **not included in any of the three lists**.

The **Governor-General**, acting in his discretion, could authorise either the federal or a provincial legislature to legislate on such a matter.

Thus, the usual revision statement is:

> **Residuary powers were vested in the Governor-General.**

**Do not assume that they automatically belonged to the federal legislature.**

---

## 6. Provincial Autonomy

The Act **abolished dyarchy in the provinces** and introduced **provincial autonomy**.

It came into operation on **1 April 1937**.

### What changed?

Under the 1919 system, provincial subjects were divided into:

- Reserved subjects.
- Transferred subjects.

The 1935 Act abolished this division at the provincial level.

### Responsible provincial ministries

- Governors were assisted by ministers.
- Ministers were responsible to the provincial legislature.
- Provincial legislatures could hold ministries politically accountable.

### Meaning of provincial autonomy

Within their constitutionally defined sphere, provinces received authority over administration, legislation and finance.

Their powers were derived from the constitutional statute rather than merely delegated by the central government.

### Financial powers

The Act provided provinces with:

- Defined revenue resources.
- Greater control over provincial expenditure.
- Borrowing powers subject to statutory conditions.

> **Provincial autonomy did not mean provincial sovereignty or freedom from British supervision.**

---

## 7. Limits on Provincial Autonomy

Governors retained substantial powers.

These included:

- Discretionary powers.
- Special responsibilities, including protection of minorities and prevention of serious threats to peace.
- Powers concerning assent to legislation.
- Powers to reserve bills for higher consideration.
- Ordinance-making powers.

### Section 93

If constitutional government in a province could not be carried on according to the Act, the governor could assume governmental functions under **Section 93**.

### Implication

Ministers were responsible to the legislature, but governors could act independently in specified areas.

> **The Act introduced responsible provincial government subject to important safeguards and overriding powers.**

### Did provincial autonomy end everywhere in 1939?

No.

- Congress ministries resigned in **1939**, protesting India’s involvement in the Second World War without consultation.
- Governor’s rule followed in several affected provinces.
- Ministries continued in some other provinces.
- The statutory framework of provincial autonomy was not universally abolished in 1939.

---

## 8. Dyarchy at the Centre: Proposed but Not Implemented

The Act provided for dyarchy in the **federal executive**.

Federal subjects were divided into:

| Category | Proposed administration |
|---|---|
| Reserved subjects | Governor-General assisted by counsellors, without responsibility to the legislature |
| Transferred subjects | Governor-General assisted by ministers responsible to the federal legislature |

### Reserved subjects

Important reserved subjects included:

1. Defence.
2. External affairs.
3. Ecclesiastical affairs.
4. Tribal areas.

**Ecclesiastical affairs** refers to church-related affairs.

### Implementation

Central dyarchy **never came into operation**, because the federation itself was not established.

The Centre continued broadly under the earlier constitutional arrangements, with modifications and transitional provisions.

### Essential comparison

| Government of India Act, 1919 | Government of India Act, 1935 |
|---|---|
| Introduced dyarchy in the provinces | Abolished provincial dyarchy |
| Did not introduce central dyarchy | Proposed central dyarchy |
| Provincial dyarchy operated | Central dyarchy never operated |

---

## 9. Proposed Federal Legislature

The Act proposed a bicameral federal legislature.

| Feature | Council of State | Federal Assembly |
|---|---|---|
| Position | Upper House | Lower House |
| Maximum membership | 260 | 375 |
| British Indian representatives | 156 | 250 |
| Princely-state representatives | Up to 104 | Up to 125 |
| Duration | Permanent chamber with periodic retirement | Normally five years |

### Selection of members

- British Indian representation in the **Council of State** included direct election under a restricted franchise and nominated members.
- British Indian representatives in the **Federal Assembly** were to be chosen indirectly through provincial assemblies.
- Princely-state representatives were to be chosen by their rulers.

### Important implications

- The proposed lower house was **not based on direct adult suffrage**.
- Representatives of princely states were **not to be elected by the people of those states**.
- These proposed federal chambers **never came into operation**.

### Legislative safeguards

The Governor-General retained powers to:

- Withhold assent.
- Reserve bills for the Crown.
- Exercise special legislative and emergency powers.

The Crown also retained powers of disallowance.

---

## 10. Bicameralism in the Provinces

The Act provided bicameral legislatures in **six of the eleven Governors’ Provinces**.

### Six bicameral provinces

1. Bengal.
2. Bombay.
3. Madras.
4. Bihar.
5. Assam.
6. United Provinces.

Each had:

- **Legislative Council**: Upper House.
- **Legislative Assembly**: Lower House.

### Five unicameral provinces

1. Punjab.
2. Central Provinces and Berar.
3. North-West Frontier Province.
4. Sind.
5. Orissa.

### Important distinction

| First introduced by | Institution |
|---|---|
| Government of India Act, 1919 | Bicameralism at the Centre |
| Government of India Act, 1935 | Bicameralism in specified provinces |

> **Trap:** Punjab did not receive a bicameral legislature under the 1935 Act.

---

## 11. Franchise and Representation

### A. Restricted franchise

The Act substantially expanded the electorate, to approximately **35 million people**.

This is commonly summarised as **about one-tenth of the population**.

Eligibility still depended on qualifications such as:

- Property.
- Tax payment.
- Education.
- Other prescribed criteria.

**Universal adult franchise was not introduced.**

### B. Communal electorates and special representation

The electoral arrangements continued communal representation and provided representation for different communities and interests.

These included:

- Muslims.
- Sikhs.
- Europeans.
- Anglo-Indians.
- Indian Christians.
- Women.
- Labour.
- Landholders.
- Commercial and other special interests.

**Do not treat every form of special representation as the same as a separate communal electorate.**

### C. Depressed Classes: Reserved seats, not separate electorates

The **Communal Award of 1932** had proposed separate electorates for the Depressed Classes.

The **Poona Pact of 1932** replaced this arrangement with **reserved seats within joint electorates**.

The electoral arrangements under the 1935 Act reflected this settlement.

- Seats were reserved for the Scheduled Castes.
- A preliminary selection process involved Scheduled Caste voters.
- The final election took place within the joint electorate.

> **Important correction:** It is inaccurate to state that the 1935 Act introduced separate electorates for the Depressed Classes.

---

## 12. Federal Court

The Act provided for a **Federal Court**, which began functioning on **1 October 1937**.

### First Chief Justice

**Sir Maurice Gwyer.**

### Jurisdiction

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Original | Specified disputes between governments |
| Appellate | Appeals, initially especially those involving interpretation of the constitutional statute |
| Advisory | Opinions on questions referred by the Governor-General |

### Position of the Privy Council

The Federal Court was not the final court of appeal in every respect.

Appeals to the **Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London** remained possible.

### Important distinction

> **The Federal Court was established even though the proposed All-India Federation was not.**

---

## 13. Public Service Commissions

The Act provided for:

1. A **Federal Public Service Commission**.
2. **Provincial Public Service Commissions**.
3. A **Joint Public Service Commission** for two or more provinces under the prescribed arrangements.

### Timeline

| Year | Development |
|---|---|
| 1926 | First Public Service Commission established in India |
| 1937 | It became the Federal Public Service Commission under the 1935 framework |
| 1950 | Union Public Service Commission under the Constitution |

> **Trap:** The 1935 Act did not create India’s first-ever Public Service Commission.

---

## 14. Secretary of State and Council of India

The Act abolished the **Council of India**, which had been established under the Government of India Act, 1858.

The Secretary of State was instead assisted by advisers.

### Important distinctions

- The **Council of India** was abolished.
- The office of the **Secretary of State for India** was not abolished.
- British governmental and parliamentary control continued.

Do not confuse the Council of India in London with legislative councils in India.

---

## 15. RBI: A Necessary Distinction

The Reserve Bank of India commenced operations on **1 April 1935**.

Its establishing statute was the:

> **Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934.**

The Government of India Act, 1935, recognised and incorporated the Bank into its constitutional and financial arrangements.

However, the precise answer to:

**“Under which Act was the RBI established?”**

is:

**Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934.**

---

## 16. Territorial Changes

| Change | Date |
|---|---|
| Sind separated from Bombay | 1936 |
| Bihar and Orissa became separate provinces | 1936 |
| Burma separated from British India under the associated constitutional settlement | 1937 |
| Aden separated from British Indian administration | 1937 |

### Important distinction

Burma’s separate constitutional arrangements were governed by the **Government of Burma Act, 1935**.

Do not confuse the dates of provincial reorganisation in **1936** with the commencement of provincial autonomy in **1937**.

---

## 17. Constitutional Limitations

The Act did not establish complete responsible government in India.

Important limitations were:

- No responsible parliamentary government at the Centre.
- Extensive powers for the Governor-General and provincial governors.
- Restricted franchise.
- Communal and special-interest representation.
- No comprehensive, enforceable bill of fundamental rights.
- No immediate grant of Dominion status.
- No power for Indian legislatures to freely amend the constitutional framework.
- Ultimate constitutional authority remained with the British Parliament.

### Indian political response

Congress rejected the Act as an adequate constitutional settlement and demanded a constitution framed by a **Constituent Assembly**.

Nevertheless, Congress contested the **1937 provincial elections** and formed ministries.

> **Rejecting the constitutional scheme did not mean boycotting the elections held under it.**

---

## 18. What Actually Came into Operation?

| Provision | Status |
|---|---|
| Provincial autonomy | Implemented in 1937 |
| Abolition of provincial dyarchy | Implemented |
| Provincial elections under the new arrangements | Held in 1937 |
| Bicameralism in six provinces | Implemented |
| Federal Court | Established in 1937 |
| Public Service Commission arrangements | Implemented |
| Proposed All-India Federation | Never established |
| Proposed central dyarchy | Never implemented |
| Proposed federal legislature of 260 and 375 members | Never constituted |

---

## 19. Continuation after Independence

The Act did not simply cease to have effect on **15 August 1947**.

Under the Indian Independence Act, 1947:

- The 1935 Act continued, with adaptations, as an interim constitutional framework.
- It operated subject to the new legal position created by independence.

In India, it was repealed by **Article 395 of the Constitution**, when the Constitution came into force on **26 January 1950**.

> **Distinguish the end of British rule in 1947 from the replacement of the adapted constitutional framework in 1950.**

---

## 20. Government of India Acts: 1919 vs 1935

| Feature | Act of 1919 | Act of 1935 |
|---|---|---|
| Provinces | Dyarchy | Provincial autonomy |
| Central dyarchy | Not provided | Provided but never implemented |
| All-India Federation with princely states | Not provided | Proposed but never established |
| Legislative subjects | Central and provincial division | Federal, Provincial and Concurrent Lists |
| Central bicameralism | Introduced | New federal bicameral scheme proposed |
| Provincial bicameralism | Not introduced | Introduced in six provinces |
| Franchise | Restricted | Expanded but still restricted |
| Public Service Commission | Provision leading to the 1926 Commission | Federal, Provincial and Joint PSC arrangements |
| Federal Court | Not provided | Provided; established in 1937 |
| Fully responsible government at the Centre | Absent | Not established |

---

## 21. Final Revision Checklist

- **321 sections and 10 schedules** in the India-only Act.
- All-India Federation **proposed, never established**.
- Princely-state accession through an **Instrument of Accession**.
- Lists: **59 Federal, 54 Provincial, 36 Concurrent**.
- Residuary powers: **Governor-General’s discretion**.
- Provincial dyarchy abolished.
- Provincial autonomy began on **1 April 1937**.
- Central dyarchy proposed but never implemented.
- Six provinces received bicameral legislatures.
- Restricted franchise continued.
- Scheduled Castes received reserved seats within joint electorates.
- Federal Court established in **1937**.
- Council of India abolished; Secretary of State retained.
- RBI established under the **RBI Act, 1934**.
- Adapted Act continued after independence.
- Repealed in India by **Article 395 in 1950**.
