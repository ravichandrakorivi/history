# Era of Militant Nationalism in India (1905–1909)

## 1. Rise of Militant Nationalism

- Militant nationalism began emerging in the 1890s and became a prominent political force by 1905.
- The Extremists rejected exclusive reliance on the Moderate methods of petitions and constitutional appeals.
- Their principal ideas were Swaraj, Swadeshi, Boycott, national education, self-reliance and passive resistance.
- They emphasised the capacity of Indians to participate in political struggles and make sacrifices.

### 1.1 Factors Responsible for Its Growth

| Factor                               | Important Prelims Facts                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Dissatisfaction with Moderates       | Criticised the methods of prayer, petition and protest as political mendicancy                  |
| Colonial economic exploitation       | Famines of 1896–1900, plague, poverty and unemployment contributed to discontent                |
| Repressive British policies          | Sedition prosecutions, press restrictions and government interference in education              |
| Growth of self-confidence            | Greater emphasis on Indian initiative and the capacity of the masses                            |
| International developments           | Ethiopia defeated Italy (1896); Boer War (1899–1902); Japan defeated Russia (1905)              |
| Cultural and intellectual influences | Swami Vivekananda, Bankim Chandra Chatterjee and Dayananda Saraswati                            |
| Lord Curzon's policies               | Calcutta Corporation Act, Indian Universities Act, Official Secrets Act and Partition of Bengal |

## 2. Partition of Bengal (1905)

### 2.1 Important Facts

| Feature                                        | Fact                                               |
| ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Viceroy                                        | Lord Curzon                                        |
| Partition proposal made public                 | December 1903                                      |
| Formal announcement                            | July 1905                                          |
| Partition implemented                          | 16 October 1905                                    |
| Official justification                         | Bengal was too large for efficient administration  |
| Political objective identified by nationalists | Weaken the nationalist movement by dividing Bengal |
| Capital of Eastern Bengal and Assam            | Dacca (Dhaka)                                      |
| Capital of the remaining Bengal province       | Calcutta                                           |
| Annulment of partition                         | 1911                                               |

The partition created Eastern Bengal and Assam, with a Muslim-majority population, while the remaining Bengal province included western Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.

### 2.2 Important Dates of the Swadeshi Movement

| Date            | Event                                                                                                       |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 7 August 1905   | Boycott Resolution adopted at Calcutta Town Hall; formal proclamation of the Swadeshi Movement              |
| 16 October 1905 | Partition came into effect; observed as a day of mourning and protest                                       |
| 1905            | Congress session at Benares supported the anti-partition and Swadeshi Movement                              |
| 1906            | Calcutta Congress session adopted Swaraj as its goal and supported Swadeshi, Boycott and national education |
| 1907            | Surat Split between Moderates and Extremists                                                                |
| By 1908         | Open phase of the Swadeshi Movement had largely declined                                                    |
| 1911            | Partition of Bengal annulled                                                                                |

### 2.3 Important Cultural Symbols

- **Raksha Bandhan:** Used to symbolise unity between the divided parts of Bengal.
- **Vande Mataram:** Became a prominent song and slogan of the movement.
- **Amar Sonar Bangla:** Composed by Rabindranath Tagore during the anti-partition movement; later became Bangladesh's national anthem.

**Prelims distinction:** The anti-partition agitation began before 1905, but the partition actually took effect on 16 October 1905.

## 3. Swadeshi and Boycott Movement

### 3.1 Important Methods and Programmes

| Programme              | Important Facts                                                     |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Swadeshi               | Promotion and use of Indian-made goods                              |
| Boycott                | Refusal to purchase foreign goods, particularly British cloth       |
| Passive resistance     | Organised non-cooperation with colonial institutions                |
| National education     | Establishment of Indian-controlled educational institutions         |
| Atma Shakti            | Self-reliance, self-confidence and national regeneration            |
| Samitis                | Volunteer organisations for mobilisation, education and social work |
| Indigenous enterprises | Promotion of Indian-owned industries, banks and shipping companies  |
| Public mobilisation    | Meetings, processions, picketing and use of festivals               |

**Important distinction:** Swadeshi meant encouraging indigenous goods and institutions, while Boycott meant rejecting foreign goods and, in the wider Extremist programme, colonial institutions.

### 3.5 Participation and Limitations

- Students: Participated in picketing, processions and propaganda.
- Women: Especially urban middle-class women, participated in public activities.
- Workers: Strikes occurred in railways, jute mills and textile industries.
- Muslims: Some leaders, including Abdul Rasul and Liaqat Hussain, participated; participation varied by region and social group.
- Peasantry: Large-scale and sustained participation remained limited.

Why the movement declined by 1908: Government repression, arrest or removal of leaders, organisational weaknesses, limited peasant participation and the Surat Split of 1907.

## 4. Moderates and Extremists — Important Differences

| Feature               | Moderates                                               | Extremists                                                             |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Principal goal        | Gradual constitutional reforms and self-government      | Swaraj as a central political goal                                     |
| Methods               | Petitions, resolutions and constitutional agitation     | Swadeshi, Boycott, passive resistance and mass mobilisation            |
| Political inspiration | Western liberal and constitutional ideas                | Indian historical and cultural traditions, alongside nationalist ideas |
| British rule          | Initially expected reforms through British institutions | Increasingly rejected faith in the benevolence of British rule         |
| Mass participation    | Primarily educated middle-class political activity      | Greater emphasis on involving the masses                               |
| Economic approach     | Criticism of economic exploitation                      | Economic self-reliance and indigenous enterprises                      |

Important qualification: Extremists were not identical in their understanding of Swaraj. For Tilak, it could mean self-government; Aurobindo advanced a more explicit demand for complete independence.

### 5.1 Surat Split (1907)

- Place: Surat, Bombay Presidency.
- Year: December 1907.
- Principal disagreement: Scope of Swadeshi and Boycott, methods of agitation and Congress leadership.
- Extremists: Wanted wider boycott and passive resistance.
- Moderates: Preferred constitutional methods and a more limited programme.
- Outcome: Congress split; Moderates retained organisational control.

Prelims trap: The split occurred in 1907 at Surat, not at the Calcutta session of 1906.

## 8. Indian Councils Act, 1909 — Morley–Minto Reforms

### 8.1 Important Personalities

| Personality              | Position                                                                        |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Lord Minto II            | Viceroy of India (1905–1910)                                                    |
| John Morley              | Secretary of State for India                                                    |
| Satyendra Prasanna Sinha | First Indian appointed to the Viceroy's Executive Council (1909), as Law Member |

### 8.2 Important Provisions

| Feature                     | Provision                                                                                                                |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Legislative councils        | Increased membership of central and provincial legislative councils                                                      |
| Central Legislative Council | Maximum additional membership increased from 16 to 60                                                                    |
| Elections                   | Expanded the electoral principle, largely through indirect elections                                                     |
| Separate electorates        | Introduced separate electorates for Muslims                                                                              |
| Muslim representation       | Provided special representation, including weightage                                                                     |
| Central council             | Official majority retained                                                                                               |
| Provincial councils         | Non-official majorities introduced, but not necessarily elected majorities                                               |
| Budget                      | Members could discuss the budget and vote on certain separate items, but not the budget as a whole                       |
| Questions                   | Supplementary questions permitted subject to rules                                                                       |
| Resolutions                 | Members could move resolutions on specified public matters                                                               |
| Executive Council           | Provision for greater Indian participation; S.P. Sinha became the first Indian member of the Viceroy's Executive Council |
| Responsible government      | Not introduced                                                                                                           |

### 8.3 Meaning of Separate Electorates

Under a separate electorate, Muslim voters elected representatives to seats designated for Muslims.

This differed from a joint electorate, where eligible voters of different communities vote in a common constituency.

### 8.4 Indian Councils Act, 1892 vs 1909

| Feature                     | Act of 1892                                                            | Act of 1909                                            |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Legislative council size    | Increased                                                              | Further increased                                      |
| Elections                   | Indirect electoral element, without explicitly using the term election | Electoral principle explicitly recognised and expanded |
| Separate Muslim electorates | No                                                                     | Yes                                                    |
| Budget discussion           | Permitted                                                              | Permitted                                              |
| Budget voting               | Not permitted                                                          | Limited voting on certain items                        |
| Supplementary questions     | Not permitted                                                          | Permitted subject to rules                             |
| Resolutions                 | No comparable general power                                            | Permitted on specified matters                         |
| Responsible government      | No                                                                     | No                                                     |

Most important Prelims fact: The Indian Councils Act, 1909 introduced separate electorates for Muslims, but did not introduce responsible government.

## Important Acts During British Rule in India (1899–1923)

## 1. Calcutta Corporation Act, 1899

- Viceroy: Lord Curzon.
- Objective: Increase British government control over municipal administration in Calcutta.
- Key provisions:
  - Reduced elected representation in the Calcutta Municipal Corporation.
  - Increased the influence of government-nominated members.
  - Restricted Indian participation in municipal self-government.
- Significance: Represented a reversal of the policy of encouraging local self-government associated with Lord Ripon.

## 2. Indian Universities Act, 1904

- Viceroy: Lord Curzon.
- Background: Recommendations of the Indian Universities Commission (1902), chaired by Sir Thomas Raleigh.
- Objective: Reform university administration and strengthen government supervision over higher education.
- Key provisions:
  - Increased government control over university senates and administration.
  - Gave the government greater influence over appointments and university regulations.
  - Strengthened university supervision of affiliated colleges.
  - Introduced stricter conditions for granting and retaining college affiliation.
  - Provided for improved teaching and research facilities.
- Significance: Criticised by Indian nationalists for reducing university autonomy and increasing official control over higher education.

## 3. Indian Official Secrets (Amendment) Act, 1904

- Viceroy: Lord Curzon.
- Objective: Strengthen the existing law protecting confidential government information and official secrets.
- Key provisions:
  - Amended the Indian Official Secrets Act, 1889 (under Lord Lansdowne, 1888–1894).
  - Strengthened legal provisions against unauthorised disclosure of official information.
  - Increased restrictions concerning government secrets.
- Significance: Strengthened colonial secrecy laws during a period of increasing nationalist political activity.
- Repealed: 1923, by the Official Secrets Act, 1923.

## 4. Official Secrets Act, 1923

- Viceroy: Lord Reading.
- Objective: Consolidate and strengthen laws relating to official secrets, espionage and unauthorised disclosure of sensitive government information.
- Key provisions:
  - Prohibited espionage and activities prejudicial to state security.
  - Restricted unauthorised communication of secret official information.
  - Protected military installations, defence establishments and other designated prohibited places.
  - Provided criminal penalties for offences under the Act.
- Earlier laws: Repealed the Indian Official Secrets Act, 1889 and its 1904 amendment.
- Present status: Continues to operate in independent India.

**Important Prelims distinction:** 

Section 22 of the **Right to Information Act, 2005** gives the RTI Act overriding effect where its provisions conflict with the **Official Secrets Act, 1923**.

## Press Acts During British Rule in India (1878–1931)

## 1. Vernacular Press Act, 1878

- Viceroy: Lord Lytton.
- Objective: Suppress criticism of British policies in Indian-language newspapers.
- Key provisions:
  - District magistrates could require printers and publishers to furnish bonds against publishing objectionable material.
  - English-language newspapers were exempted.
  - No right to appeal against the magistrate's action in a court of law.
- Popular name: Gagging Act.
- Repealed: 1882, by Lord Ripon.

## 2. Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act, 1908

- Viceroy: Lord Minto II.
- Objective: Suppress revolutionary publications that incited murder, violence and offences involving explosives.
- Key provisions:
  - Magistrates could order the confiscation of printing presses used for publishing material inciting specified offences.
  - Provision for seizure of objectionable publications and attachment of press property.
  - Affected publishers could challenge forfeiture orders before the High Court, subject to statutory restrictions.
- Associated newspapers: Yugantar, Sandhya and Bande Mataram faced colonial repression during this period.
- Important distinction: Bal Gangadhar Tilak was sentenced to six years' imprisonment in Mandalay in 1908 for sedition relating to writings in Kesari. His conviction was under sedition law, not specifically under this Act.
- Repealed: 1922, following the recommendations of the Press Committee chaired by Tej Bahadur Sapru.

## 3. Indian Press Act, 1910

- Viceroy: Lord Minto II.
- Objective: Control nationalist and revolutionary publications, particularly during the period following the Swadeshi Movement.
- Key provisions:
  - Required printers and publishers to furnish financial security deposits.
  - Security could be forfeited for publishing material considered objectionable or seditious.
  - Authorities could seize publications and printing presses.
  - Strengthened government control over the circulation of nationalist literature.
- Repealed: 1922, following the recommendations of the Tej Bahadur Sapru Press Committee (1921).

## 4. Indian Press (Emergency Powers) Act, 1931

- Viceroy: Lord Willingdon.
- Background: Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–34).
- Objective: Suppress nationalist propaganda and publications encouraging civil disobedience or revolutionary activities.
- Key provisions:
  - Authorities could demand security deposits from printers and publishers.
  - Security deposits could be forfeited for publishing objectionable material.
  - Provincial governments could seize publications and take action against printing presses.
  - Restrictions extended to publications encouraging violence and certain forms of political resistance.
- 1932: Subsequent amendments strengthened the government's powers against nationalist publications.
- World War II: Additional censorship was imposed under the Defence of India Rules, 1939.
- Repealed: 1951, by the Press (Objectionable Matter) Act, 1951 (Act 56 of 1951).
