# Debates after the Civil Disobedience Movement, Government of India Act, 1935 and Congress Provincial Ministries (1937–1939)


## 1. Background: Withdrawal of Civil Disobedience Movement

- The Civil Disobedience Movement was withdrawn in April 1934.
- Congress faced the question of how to continue the freedom struggle when immediate mass mobilisation was not feasible.
- Two major debates followed:

| Stage  | Period  | Central question                                                                                     |
| ------ | ------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| First  | 1934–35 | What strategy should Congress adopt after the withdrawal of the Civil Disobedience Movement?         |
| Second | 1937    | Should Congress form provincial governments after elections under the Government of India Act, 1935? |

## 2. First Stage Debate: Future Strategy (1934–35)

Three approaches emerged within Congress.

| Approach                      | Supporters                                                       | Principal argument                                                                                       |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Gandhian constructive work    | Gandhi and his supporters                                        | Continue social and constructive activities during the absence of mass struggle                          |
| Council entry                 | M.A. Ansari, Asaf Ali, Bhulabhai Desai, S. Satyamurthy, B.C. Roy | Participate in elections and legislatures to sustain political activity and prepare for future struggles |
| Continuation of mass struggle | Jawaharlal Nehru and Congress leftists                           | Avoid replacing anti-imperialist mass struggle with parliamentary politics                               |

### 2.1 Arguments supporting council entry

Supporters argued that:

- Legislative work could sustain political interest during a period of reduced mass activity.
- Participation in elections did not necessarily imply acceptance of British constitutional arrangements.
- Legislatures could provide an additional platform for nationalist activity.
- Council work could strengthen Congress organisation before the next mass movement.

### 2.2 Nehru's socialist approach

Nehru argued for:

- Ending capitalism and establishing socialism.
- Incorporating workers' and peasants' economic demands into the national movement.
- Organising workers through trade unions and peasants through kisan sabhas.
- Allowing these class organisations to affiliate with Congress.
- Continuing direct anti-imperialist struggle rather than relying on constitutional politics.

Prelims distinction: Nehru opposed council entry as a substitute for mass struggle, not because he rejected all forms of political organisation.

## 3. Struggle–Truce–Struggle versus Struggle–Victory

| Feature                   | Struggle–Truce–Struggle (S–T–S)                           | Struggle–Victory (S–V)                                  |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Associated with           | Gandhi and many Congress leaders                          | Jawaharlal Nehru                                        |
| Strategy                  | Alternating phases of mass struggle and political respite | Continuous confrontation with imperialism until victory |
| Purpose of interval       | Allow masses to recover and reorganise                    | Avoid weakening revolutionary momentum                  |
| View of mass mobilisation | Cannot continue indefinitely without periods of recovery  | Should remain central and continuous                    |

Gandhi's approach recognised that prolonged mass struggle could exhaust participants. Nehru believed the movement should maintain continuous pressure after the adoption of Purna Swaraj.

## 4. Congress Decision on Council Entry, 1934

- May 1934: The All India Congress Committee met at Patna.
- A Parliamentary Board was established to contest elections under the Congress organisation.
- Gandhi accepted council entry as a permissible activity for Congressmen, while warning against excessive dependence on constitutional politics.
- October 1934: Gandhi resigned from the Congress organisation.
- Nehru and the socialists remained within Congress, preferring to influence it rather than create a separate mass organisation.

# Government of India Act, 1935

## 5. Background and Significance

The Act was passed by the British Parliament in August 1935.

Its constitutional background included:

- Simon Commission Report, 1930.
- Three Round Table Conferences, 1930–32.
- White Paper on Indian Constitutional Reforms, 1933.
- Examination by a Joint Select Committee of the British Parliament.

### Principal features

1. Proposed All-India Federation.
2. Dyarchy proposed at the Centre.
3. Provincial autonomy replacing provincial dyarchy.
4. Bicameral federal legislature.
5. Three legislative lists.
6. Extension of the franchise.
7. Continuation and extension of communal representation.
8. Extensive discretionary powers and safeguards for governors and the governor-general.

Important: The proposed All-India Federation was never established. Provincial autonomy, however, came into operation in 1937.

## 6. Proposed All-India Federation

The federation was to comprise:

- British Indian provinces.
- Chief Commissioners' provinces.
- Indian princely states.

### Conditions for formation

The accession of princely states had to satisfy both conditions:

1. Acceding states had to be entitled to at least 52 seats in the proposed Council of States.
2. Their combined population had to constitute at least 50% of the population of all Indian states.

These conditions were not fulfilled.

Result: The federation never came into existence.

| Unit                     | Entry into proposed federation |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| British Indian provinces | Compulsory                     |
| Princely states          | Voluntary, through accession   |

Prelims distinction: The federal provisions of the Act were proposed but not implemented. The Centre continued substantially under the earlier constitutional arrangements.

## 7. Dyarchy at the Centre

The Act proposed dividing federal executive subjects into two categories.

| Reserved subjects      | Transferred subjects                      |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Defence                | Subjects other than the reserved subjects |
| External affairs       |                                           |
| Ecclesiastical affairs |                                           |
| Tribal areas           |                                           |

### Administration

Reserved subjects

- Administered by the Governor-General with the assistance of executive councillors.
- Executive councillors were not responsible to the legislature.

Transferred subjects

- Administered by the Governor-General with the assistance of ministers.
- Ministers were to be responsible to the federal legislature.

Important distinction:

- Government of India Act, 1919: Dyarchy introduced in provinces.
- Government of India Act, 1935: Provincial dyarchy abolished; dyarchy proposed at the Centre.

Because the federation was not established, the proposed federal dyarchy was not implemented.

## 8. Proposed Federal Legislature

The Act proposed a bicameral legislature.

| Feature                         | Council of States           | Federal Assembly                        |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Position                        | Upper House                 | Lower House                             |
| Total membership                | 260                         | 375                                     |
| Princely states' representation | 104 seats                   | 125 seats                               |
| British Indian representation   | 156 seats                   | 250 seats                               |
| Election from British India     | Direct                      | Indirect                                |
| Tenure                          | Permanent House             | Five years                              |
| Retirement                      | One-third every three years | Dissolution at end of tenure or earlier |

Important: Princely-state representatives were to be nominated by rulers rather than elected through a popular franchise.

### Other provisions

- The Federal Assembly could pass a vote of no-confidence against ministers responsible to it.
- The Council of States could not remove ministers through a no-confidence vote.
- The Governor-General retained extensive legislative and financial powers.
- A substantial portion of expenditure remained outside legislative voting.

These federal arrangements were not brought into operation.

## 9. Division of Legislative Powers

The Act provided three legislative lists.

| List            | Number of subjects | Legislative authority   |
| --------------- | ------------------ | ----------------------- |
| Federal List    | 59                 | Federal legislature     |
| Provincial List | 54                 | Provincial legislatures |
| Concurrent List | 36                 | Both                    |

### Residuary powers

- Residuary legislative powers were assigned to the Governor-General.
- They were not automatically vested in the provinces.

Prelims distinction: Under the Constitution of independent India, residuary legislative powers belong to Parliament. Under the 1935 Act, they were vested in the Governor-General.

## 10. Powers of the Governor-General

The Governor-General remained the central authority under the proposed constitutional structure.

Important powers included:

- Special responsibilities concerning peace, security and financial stability.
- Exercise of discretionary and individual-judgment powers.
- Veto over legislation.
- Promulgation of ordinances.
- Certification of bills rejected by the legislature.
- Restoration of reductions made in certain grants.
- Control over reserved subjects under the proposed federal dyarchy.

Important: The Act did not establish fully responsible government at the Centre.

## 11. Provincial Autonomy

This was the most important part of the Act actually implemented.

### Major changes

- Dyarchy was abolished in the provinces.
- Provincial ministers were made responsible to elected provincial legislatures.
- Provinces received a distinct constitutional and financial position.
- Provincial governments could borrow money on their own security.
- Ministers administered provincial subjects, subject to the Governor's special powers.

### Powers retained by the Governor

- Discretionary powers and special responsibilities.
- Protection of minority interests and civil servants.
- Responsibility for peace and tranquillity.
- Power to withhold assent from legislation.
- Power to promulgate ordinances.
- Authority to assume direct administration under specified circumstances.

Thus, provincial autonomy was limited, not complete.

## 12. Franchise and Representation

- Franchise was substantially expanded but remained restricted by qualifications.
- The textbook estimates that approximately 14% of the population was enfranchised.
- Women could vote subject to applicable qualifications.
- Separate electorates continued and were extended under communal representation arrangements.
- The Act did not introduce universal adult franchise.

### Other important provisions of the 1935 Act

The Act also provided for:

- Establishment of a Federal Court, which began functioning in 1937.
- Establishment of a Federal Public Service Commission and provincial public service commissions.
- Separation of Burma from British India, effective in 1937.
- Separation of Sind from Bombay Presidency.
- Establishment of Orissa as a separate province.

## 13. Limitations and Nationalist Response

### Major criticisms

- The Governor-General retained extensive powers.
- Governors could override elected provincial ministries in specified matters.
- The proposed federation gave substantial representation to princely states without democratic elections.
- The Act retained communal electorates.
- The franchise remained restricted.
- Constitutional amendment remained under the control of the British Parliament.

Jawaharlal Nehru famously described the Act as:

> A car, all brakes and no engine.

### Congress response

- Congress rejected the Act as an adequate constitutional settlement.
- It demanded a Constituent Assembly elected on the basis of adult franchise to frame India's constitution.
- Nevertheless, Congress decided to contest provincial elections under the Act.

Prelims distinction: Rejection of the Act did not mean boycott of the 1937 provincial elections.

# Provincial Elections of 1937

## 14. Second Stage Debate: Office Acceptance

After deciding to contest the elections, Congress debated whether it should form ministries if it won.

### Opponents of office acceptance

Prominent opponents included:

- Jawaharlal Nehru.
- Subhas Chandra Bose.
- Congress socialists.
- Communists.

Their arguments:

- Accepting office could imply cooperation with the rejected Act.
- Governors retained powers that restricted elected ministries.
- Constitutional administration might weaken mass struggle.
- Congress should use legislatures to obstruct the Act rather than administer it.

### Supporters of office acceptance

They argued:

- Legislative office could be used as a temporary tactic.
- Ministries could introduce limited reforms.
- Congress could demonstrate its administrative capacity.
- Refusal to form ministries could leave power to pro-government forces.
- Office acceptance did not mean abandoning independence.

### Gandhi's position

- Initially opposed office acceptance.
- Later agreed to allow Congress ministries to be formed as an experiment.

- First Congress annual session held in a rural setting.
- Held in present-day Maharashtra.
- Reflected the increasing importance of agrarian questions within Congress politics.

### Congress election manifesto

The manifesto promised:

- Release of political prisoners.
- Removal of disabilities based on caste and gender.
- Agrarian reform.
- Reduction of land revenue and rent.
- Relief from rural indebtedness.
- Cheaper agricultural credit.
- Trade union rights and the right to strike.

# Congress Provincial Ministries (1937–1939)

## 18. Formation of Ministries

Congress eventually formed ministries in eight provinces:

| Province                    | Prominent Premier / Leader            |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Madras                      | C. Rajagopalachari                    |
| Bombay                      | B.G. Kher                             |
| United Provinces            | Govind Ballabh Pant                   |
| Bihar                       | Shri Krishna Sinha                    |
| Central Provinces and Berar | N.B. Khare, later Ravi Shankar Shukla |
| Orissa                      | Biswanath Das                         |
| NWFP                        | Dr Khan Sahib                         |
| Assam                       | Gopinath Bordoloi                     |

Prelims distinction: Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan led the Khudai Khidmatgar movement, while his brother Dr Khan Sahib headed the Congress ministry in the NWFP.

Congress did not form ministries in Bengal, Punjab or Sind.

## 19. Gandhi's Advice to Congress Ministers

Gandhi advised ministers to:

- Treat office as a responsibility rather than a privilege.
- Use their limited authority to advance nationalist objectives.
- Avoid dependence on colonial administrative methods.
- Demonstrate that Indians could govern effectively.

He described ministerial offices as crowns of thorns.

The ministries were expected to use constitutional office without abandoning the struggle for independence.

## 20. Work of Congress Ministries: Civil Liberties

Important measures included:

- Release of political prisoners.
- Withdrawal of several restrictions on newspapers.
- Removal of bans on certain books and journals.
- Lifting restrictions on organisations such as the Hindustan Seva Dal and youth leagues.
- Revocation of certain internment and deportation orders.
- Reduction of police surveillance of political workers.
- Restoration of confiscated lands in Bombay.
- Restoration of pensions in certain cases connected with the Civil Disobedience Movement.

### Limitations

The ministries were criticised for actions against some socialists and communists.

Important personalities:

- Yusuf Meherally: Arrested and later released in Madras.
- S.S. Batliwala: Arrested in Madras for a speech considered seditious.
- K.M. Munshi: As Bombay Home Minister, criticised for using the CID against leftists.

Prelims significance: Congress ministries expanded civil liberties but did not entirely abandon colonial policing and restrictive laws.

## 21. Agrarian Reforms

Congress ministries introduced measures concerning:

- Reduction of rents and land revenue burdens.
- Relief from agricultural indebtedness.
- Regulation of moneylending.
- Tenancy protection.
- Reduction of forest grazing fees.
- Arrears of rent and land tenure issues.

### Limitations

- Governors retained important powers.
- Financial resources were inadequate.
- Landlords and other influential groups resisted reforms.
- Legislative Councils in some provinces obstructed legislation.
- The agrarian structure was complex.
- Agricultural labourers and sub-tenants benefited less than statutory and occupancy tenants.

Important: Congress ministries did not abolish zamindari during 1937–39.

## 22. Labour Policy

The ministries sought to improve workers' conditions while maintaining industrial peace.

### Main approach

- Encourage negotiations between workers and employers.
- Use conciliation and arbitration to resolve disputes.
- Promote wage improvements.
- Discourage prolonged or militant strikes.

### Criticism

- Militant labour demonstrations were sometimes treated as law-and-order problems.
- Section 144 and arrests were used in some cases.
- Leftist leaders criticised the ministries for excessive accommodation of industrialists.

### Bombay Trade Disputes Act, 1938

- Associated with the Congress ministry in Bombay.
- Imposed restrictions and procedures affecting industrial disputes and strikes.
- Criticised by trade unions and leftist organisations.

Prelims distinction: The Congress ministries were not uniformly anti-labour or uniformly supportive of strikes. Their policy combined labour welfare with industrial peace and restrictions on militant action.

## 23. Social Welfare and Educational Measures

The ministries undertook:

- Prohibition in selected areas.
- Temple-entry and anti-untouchability measures.
- Scholarships and educational assistance for disadvantaged communities.
- Improvement of primary, technical and higher education.
- Public health and sanitation measures.
- Encouragement of khadi and indigenous enterprises.
- Prison reforms.

### National Planning Committee, 1938

- Established under the Congress presidency of Subhas Chandra Bose.
- Jawaharlal Nehru became its chairman.
- It examined economic development through planned national reconstruction.

Prelims distinction: The National Planning Committee of 1938 was different from the Planning Commission of independent India, established in 1950.

## 24. Extra-Parliamentary Activities

Congress activity was not confined to provincial governments.

It included:

- Mass literacy campaigns.
- Congress-organised panchayats.
- Grievance committees presenting public petitions.
- Support for people's movements in princely states.

### Praja Mandal movements

- Associated with demands for responsible government and civil liberties in princely states.
- Created a strategic dilemma for Congress regarding intervention in princely-state affairs.

## 25. Muslim League and the Pirpur Committee, 1938

- Established by the All India Muslim League.
- Examined allegations concerning the treatment of Muslims under Congress provincial ministries.
- Its report criticised Congress governments on matters such as:
  - Religious practices.
  - Language policy, especially Hindi and Urdu.
  - Political representation.
  - Economic discrimination.

Important distinction: These were allegations advanced in a politically contested report, not findings that should automatically be treated as established facts.

## 26. Resignation of Congress Ministries, 1939

- The Second World War began in September 1939.
- Viceroy Lord Linlithgow declared India to be at war without consulting elected Indian representatives.
- Congress demanded clarification of British war aims and India's political future.
- With no satisfactory response, Congress ministries resigned in October–November 1939.

### Subsequent development

\- Muhammad Ali Jinnah called upon Muslims to observe 22 December 1939 as the Day of Deliverance following the resignation of Congress ministries.

Prelims distinction: Congress ministries resigned because of the constitutional and political dispute arising from India's entry into the Second World War, not because Congress lost the 1937 electoral mandate.
