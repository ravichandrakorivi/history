# Emergence of Mahatma Gandhi (1893–1919)

## 1. Historical Background: Emergence of Mass Nationalism

After the First World War (1914–1918), the Indian national movement increasingly moved towards mass participation under Mahatma Gandhi's leadership.

### 1.1 Factors Responsible for the Nationalist Resurgence

| Factor                            | Explanation                                                                                          |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Post-war economic hardship        | Inflation, heavy taxation, unemployment, industrial recession and agricultural distress              |
| Expectations of political reforms | Indian cooperation in the war created expectations of substantial constitutional concessions         |
| Disillusionment with imperialism  | Post-war peace settlements failed to fulfil expectations of self-determination for colonised peoples |
| Russian Revolution of 1917        | Demonstrated the political power of organised mass mobilisation                                      |
| British repressive policies       | The Rowlatt Act of 1919 intensified nationalist opposition                                           |
| Inadequate constitutional reforms | The Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms did not establish responsible government at the Centre                |

Prelims facts:

- The Bolshevik Revolution occurred on 7 November 1917 according to the Gregorian calendar.
- It is called the October Revolution because Russia then used the Julian calendar.
- The Bolsheviks, led by Vladimir Lenin, overthrew the Provisional Government, not the Tsarist government. The Tsar had already abdicated earlier in 1917.

## 3. Gandhi in South Africa (1893–1914)

### 3.1 Indian Community in South Africa

The Indian population included:

1. Indentured labourers, many employed on plantations and in mines.
2. Indian merchants and traders.
3. Former indentured labourers who had settled permanently with their families.

They faced restrictions on voting rights, residence, movement, registration and other civil liberties.

- Influenced by John Ruskin's Unto This Last.

- Served as a residential and educational centre for Satyagrahis and their families.

## 4. Major Satyagraha Campaigns in South Africa

### 4.1 Resistance to Registration Certificates (1906)

- Transvaal legislation required Indians to register and provide identifying details, including fingerprints.
- Gandhi organised resistance against compulsory registration.
- Protesters refused registration, accepted imprisonment and publicly burned registration certificates.
- Gandhi formed the Passive Resistance Association to organise the campaign.

This marked the beginning of Gandhi's organised Satyagraha.

### 4.2 Resistance to Immigration Restrictions

- Indians opposed discriminatory restrictions on movement between South African provinces.
- Protesters crossed provincial boundaries without complying with the required permits.
- Participants accepted arrest and imprisonment.

### 4.3 Campaign Against the £3 Tax and Marriage Restrictions (1913)

Major grievances included:

- A £3 annual tax imposed on certain former indentured Indian labourers.
- Non-recognition of marriages performed according to Indian religious customs.
- Restrictions on Indian migration and movement.

The movement involved Indian workers, women, miners and plantation labourers.

### 4.4 Settlement of 1914

The settlement associated with Gandhi and General Jan Smuts led to the Indian Relief Act, 1914.

Important concessions included:

- Abolition of the £3 tax.
- Recognition of Indian marriages under specified conditions.
- Relief from certain discriminatory requirements.

Associated personalities: C.F. Andrews, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Lord Hardinge and General Smuts.

The settlement did not abolish racial discrimination in South Africa.

## 5. Gandhian Satyagraha: Meaning and Principles

### 5.1 Meaning

Satyagraha means firm adherence to truth.

It combined truth, non-violence, moral discipline and willingness to suffer in resistance to injustice.

### 5.2 Fundamental Principles

| Principle          | Meaning                                                      |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Satya              | Truth                                                        |
| Ahimsa             | Non-violence                                                 |
| Self-suffering     | Willingness to accept hardship rather than inflict suffering |
| Fearlessness       | Refusal to submit to injustice out of fear                   |
| Non-cooperation    | Withdrawal of cooperation from unjust authority              |
| Civil disobedience | Open and non-violent violation of an unjust law              |
| Purity of means    | The objective does not justify immoral methods               |
| Absence of hatred  | Opposition to injustice without hatred towards the opponent  |

### 5.3 Satyagraha and Passive Resistance

| Feature                   | Satyagraha                                                        | Passive Resistance                                                 |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Basic principle           | Resistance based on truth and non-violence                        | Resistance primarily through non-violent or non-aggressive methods |
| Nature of non-violence    | Non-violence is a fundamental moral principle                     | Non-violence may be adopted as a practical strategy                |
| Self-suffering            | Willingness to accept suffering is essential                      | Self-suffering is not necessarily essential                        |
| Attitude towards opponent | Opposes injustice without hatred towards the opponent             | Does not necessarily require moral transformation of the opponent  |
| Objective                 | To resist injustice and persuade the opponent through moral force | Primarily to secure political or practical concessions             |
| Use of violence           | Rejected as a method of struggle                                  | Not necessarily rejected as an absolute principle                  |

Prelims fact: Gandhi considered Satyagraha different from ordinary passive resistance because it was founded on truth, non-violence, self-suffering and moral persuasion, rather than merely avoiding physical confrontation.

## 8. Champaran Satyagraha (1917)

![Chmparan Satyagraha](images/champaran_satyagraha.jpeg)

### 8.1 Basic Facts

| Feature                   | Fact                               |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Year                      | 1917                               |
| Location                  | Champaran, Bihar                   |
| Main issue                | Exploitation of indigo cultivators |
| System opposed            | Tinkathia                          |
| Person who invited Gandhi | **Raj Kumar Shukla**                   |
| Significance              | **Gandhi's first Satyagraha in India** |

### 8.2 Tinkathia System

- **Compulsory indigo cultivation:** Under the tinkathia system, European planters compelled peasants in Champaran to cultivate indigo on **3/20 (15%) of their landholdings**.
- **Exploitation of peasants:** Cultivators were required to supply indigo to European planters at very low prices, often under unfavourable contractual conditions.
- **Impact of synthetic dyes:** The development of synthetic dyes in Germany reduced the demand for natural indigo, making indigo cultivation less profitable for European planters.
- **Additional financial burden:** As demand for natural indigo declined, planters sought payments from peasants to release them from their obligations to cultivate indigo.
- **Peasant grievances:** Compulsory cultivation, low payments and additional financial demands led to widespread resentment among cultivators, forming the background to the **Champaran Satyagraha of 1917**.

### 8.3 Gandhi's Intervention

1. **Raj Kumar Shukla persuaded Gandhi to visit Champaran**.
2. Gandhi investigated the grievances of indigo cultivators.
3. Local authorities ordered him to leave the district.
4. Gandhi refused to obey the order and was prepared to face punishment.
5. The government subsequently appointed an inquiry committee that included Gandhi.
6. Reforms followed, leading to the abolition of the tinkathia system.

### 8.4 Important Associates

- **Raj Kumar Shukla**
- **Rajendra Prasad**
- **Brajkishore Prasad**
- **J.B. Kripalani**
- Mazharul Haque
- Mahadev Desai
- Narhari Parekh
- Anugrah Narayan Sinha
- Ramnavmi Prasad

### 8.5 Outcome

- The planters agreed to refund 25% of certain sums improperly collected from cultivators.
- The Champaran Agrarian Act, 1918 helped abolish the tinkathia system.

Prelims trap: The 25% figure concerns a refund of money collected by planters, not a reduction of land revenue.

## 10. Kheda Satyagraha (1918)

![Kheda Satyagraha](images/kheda_satyagraha.jpeg)

### 10.1 Basic Facts

| Feature                | Fact                                         |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Year                   | 1918 (Mar-Jun)                                        |
| Location               | Kheda district, Gujarat                     |
| Main issue             | Land-revenue collection despite crop failure |
| Important organisation | Gujarat Sabha                                |
| Principal leaders      | **Gandhi** and **Vallabhbhai Patel**                 |
| Method                 | Collective withholding of land revenue       |

### 10.2 Background

- **Crop failure:** In 1918, drought and crop failure caused severe agricultural distress in Kheda district of Gujarat.
- **Revenue rules:** Under the prevailing land-revenue rules, peasants were entitled to remission of land revenue when agricultural produce fell below **one-fourth (25%) of the normal yield**.
- **Peasants' demand:** The affected cultivators demanded suspension of land-revenue collection because they were unable to pay.
- **Role of Gujarat Sabha:** The Gujarat Sabha submitted petitions to the provincial authorities seeking revenue relief.
- **Government's response:** The authorities refused to grant the requested relief and threatened to confiscate the property of peasants who failed to pay land revenue.

### 10.3 Gandhi's Intervention

- **Organisation of Satyagraha:** Gandhi supported the peasants and advised them to withhold land-revenue payments until their demand for relief was accepted.
- **Leadership of Vallabhbhai Patel:** Patel, along with Mohanlal Pandya, Narhari Parikh and Ravi Shankar Vyas, organised peasants across villages and coordinated the agitation.
- **Non-violent resistance:** Peasants collectively refused to pay land revenue while maintaining discipline and non-violence.
- **Government repression:** The authorities confiscated land, livestock and other property belonging to some peasants who refused payment.
- **Outcome:** The government eventually relaxed revenue collection, particularly for peasants unable to pay, bringing the Satyagraha to an end.

### 10.4 Important Associates

- **Vallabhbhai Patel**
- Mohanlal Pandya
- **Narhari Parikh**
- Ravi Shankar Vyas
- Indulal Yagnik

### 10.5 Outcome

- The authorities relaxed revenue collection, particularly for peasants unable to pay.
- The movement demonstrated the effectiveness of disciplined collective action.

- Gandhi generally supported the British war effort.

## 13. Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms and Government of India Act, 1919

### 13.1 Background

| Development               | Year           | Significance                                                                         |
| ------------------------- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Montagu Declaration       | 20 August 1917 | Announced the British policy of gradually developing responsible government in India |
| Montagu–Chelmsford Report | 1918           | Recommended constitutional reforms, including provincial dyarchy                     |
| Government of India Act   | 1919           | Gave legislative effect to the proposed constitutional reforms                       |

**Important personalities:**

- **Edwin Samuel Montagu:** Secretary of State for India.
- **Lord Chelmsford:** Viceroy of India (1916–1921).

### Montagu Declaration (1917)

- Announced the gradual development of self-governing institutions and the progressive realisation of responsible government in India.
- Responsible government was to be developed **within the British Empire**, not through immediate independence.
- The British government retained control over the pace and extent of constitutional reforms.

**Meaning of responsible government:** A system in which the executive is politically accountable to the elected legislature and must retain its confidence to remain in office.

### 13.2 Main Features of the Government of India Act, 1919

The Act introduced the following important changes:

1. Divided legislative subjects into **central and provincial subjects**.
2. Introduced **dyarchy in the provinces**.
3. Expanded provincial legislative councils and increased elected representation.
4. Introduced a **bicameral legislature at the Centre**.
5. Extended communal and special representation.
6. Retained substantial powers in the hands of the Governor-General and provincial Governors.

**Important distinction:** Dyarchy was introduced at the **provincial level**, not at the Centre.

### 13.3 Provincial Dyarchy

**Meaning of dyarchy:** A system of dual administration in which provincial subjects were divided into two categories—reserved and transferred—and administered by different authorities.

| Feature                       | Reserved Subjects                                                     | Transferred Subjects                                                                             |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Examples                      | Police, finance, land revenue, irrigation, administration of justice  | Education, public health, agriculture, local self-government, industries, excise                 |
| Administered by               | Governor with executive councillors                                   | Governor with Indian ministers                                                                   |
| Appointment                   | Executive councillors appointed under the constitutional arrangements | Ministers appointed by the Governor, normally from elected members of the provincial legislature |
| Responsibility to legislature | Not responsible                                                       | Ministers were responsible to the provincial legislature                                         |
| Governor's authority          | Direct control                                                        | Retained overriding powers                                                                       |

**How the system worked:**

- **Reserved subjects:** Administered by the Governor and his executive councillors without being responsible to the provincial legislative council.
- **Transferred subjects:** Administered by ministers who were responsible to the provincial legislative council.
- The Governor could intervene in the administration of transferred subjects and retained significant control over provincial administration.

**Why dyarchy was criticised:**

- Ministers administering transferred subjects depended on financial resources controlled largely through the reserved side of government.
- Important departments such as police and finance remained outside the control of responsible ministers.
- The division created difficulties in coordination and accountability.
- The Governor could override ministers, limiting their effective authority.

### 13.4 Provincial Legislatures

- Provincial legislative councils were enlarged.
- Elected members constituted approximately **70% of provincial legislative council membership** under the reform scheme.
- The franchise was restricted by qualifications such as property, taxation and other prescribed conditions.
- The Act did not establish universal adult franchise.
- Women's voting rights were introduced through subsequent provincial arrangements, subject to prescribed qualifications.
- Provincial legislatures could discuss budgets, ask questions and exercise limited legislative control.
- Governors retained powers to assent to, reserve or withhold assent from legislation and to override the legislature in specified circumstances.

### 13.5 Central Government

The Government of India Act, 1919 **did not introduce responsible government at the Centre**.

- The Governor-General remained the head of the central executive.
- The Governor-General's Executive Council was not collectively responsible to the central legislature.
- The Governor-General retained substantial legislative, administrative and financial powers.
- Central and provincial legislative subjects were separately identified.
- The central government retained control over major subjects such as defence, foreign affairs and important financial matters.

### 13.6 Bicameral Central Legislature

The Act introduced a two-House central legislature.

| Feature                                   | Council of State | Legislative Assembly |
| ----------------------------------------- | ---------------- | -------------------- |
| Position                                  | Upper House      | Lower House          |
| Total membership under the initial scheme | 60               | 145                  |
| Elected members                           | 34               | 104                  |
| Nominated members                         | 26               | 41                   |
| Normal term                               | 5 years          | 3 years              |

**Powers and limitations:**

- Members could ask questions, debate legislation and discuss financial proposals.
- The legislature exercised limited control over the budget.
- A substantial portion of central expenditure was not subject to an effective legislative vote.
- The Governor-General could override legislative decisions through powers provided by the Act.

**Prelims distinction:** Bicameralism at the Centre did not mean that the Governor-General's Executive Council became responsible to the legislature.

### 13.7 Electoral Representation and Franchise

- The Act continued and extended **separate communal electorates**, including representation for Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans.
- Special representation was provided for certain interests.
- Voting rights were restricted to persons satisfying prescribed qualifications.
- The vast majority of Indians remained outside the electorate.

**Meaning of a separate electorate:** Members of a specified community elected representatives from that community through a separate electoral arrangement.

### 13.8 Other Important Provisions

- The salary of the Secretary of State for India was charged to the **British Exchequer**, rather than Indian revenues.
- The Act provided for the establishment of a **Public Service Commission** in India.
- The **Central Public Service Commission** was subsequently established in 1926.
- The Act provided for a statutory commission to review the working of the constitutional system after ten years. The **Simon Commission was appointed in 1927**, before the completion of that period.

### 13.9 Limitations of the Reforms

| Limitation                              | Explanation                                                                               |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| No responsible government at the Centre | The central executive was not accountable to the elected legislature                      |
| Provincial dyarchy                      | Important subjects remained under executive councillors rather than responsible ministers |
| Limited ministerial powers              | Ministers lacked effective control over finance and important administrative departments  |
| Restricted franchise                    | Only a small proportion of the population could vote                                      |
| Governor's overriding powers            | Governors could intervene in provincial administration and legislation                    |
| Limited legislative control             | The Governor-General retained extensive legislative and financial authority               |
| Continued communal electorates          | Electoral representation remained divided along communal lines                            |

### 13.10 Congress Reaction

- The Indian National Congress considered the proposed reforms inadequate.
- A **special Congress session was held at Bombay in August 1918**, presided over by **Hasan Imam**.
- Congress demanded more substantial progress towards responsible government.

### 13.11 Comparison with the Government of India Act, 1935

| Feature               | Government of India Act, 1919 | Government of India Act, 1935                                                     |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Provincial dyarchy    | Introduced                    | Abolished                                                                         |
| Provincial autonomy   | Not introduced                | Introduced                                                                        |
| Dyarchy at the Centre | Not introduced                | Proposed but never implemented                                                    |
| Central bicameralism  | Introduced                    | Continued under the existing arrangements; a new federal legislature was proposed |
| Federation            | Not established               | All-India Federation proposed but never established                               |

### 13.12 Important UPSC Prelims Facts

- **Montagu Declaration:** 20 August 1917.
- **Montagu–Chelmsford Report:** 1918.
- **Government of India Act:** 1919.
- **Provincial dyarchy:** Introduced by the 1919 Act.
- **Reserved subjects:** Finance, police, land revenue, irrigation and administration of justice.
- **Transferred subjects:** Education, public health, agriculture, local self-government, industries and excise.
- **Central legislature:** Council of State and Legislative Assembly.
- **Responsible government at the Centre:** Not introduced.
- **Franchise:** Restricted; no universal adult franchise.
- **Provincial autonomy:** Introduced later by the Government of India Act, 1935.
- **Central Public Service Commission:** Established in 1926.
- **Simon Commission:** Appointed in 1927 to review the constitutional system.

## 14. Rowlatt Act, 1919

### 14.1 Background

| Feature | Fact |
|---|---|
| Official name | **Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919** |
| Popular name | Rowlatt Act |
| Year | 1919 |
| Committee | Sedition Committee (1918), popularly known as the Rowlatt Committee |
| Chairman | Justice Sidney Rowlatt |
| Main objective | To continue extraordinary powers for suppressing revolutionary and political activities |

- During the First World War, the British government exercised extraordinary powers under the **Defence of India Act, 1915**.
- As the war ended, the government sought to retain special powers to deal with revolutionary activities.
- The **Sedition Committee**, headed by **Justice Sidney Rowlatt**, recommended measures to suppress revolutionary movements.
- Based on these recommendations, the **Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act was passed in March 1919**, despite strong opposition from Indian members of the Imperial Legislative Council.

### 14.2 Important Provisions

The Act authorised extraordinary measures against persons suspected of involvement in revolutionary activities.

- **Preventive detention:** Authorities could detain suspects without an ordinary criminal trial, subject to the Act's provisions.
- **Special courts:** Certain offences could be tried by special tribunals consisting of three High Court judges.
- **Restricted judicial safeguards:** Trials before these tribunals did not provide the normal right of appeal.
- **Restrictions on individuals:** Authorities could impose restrictions on the movement and activities of persons suspected of involvement in revolutionary activities.

**Why was it criticised?**

- It allowed extraordinary restrictions on personal liberty without the safeguards ordinarily available in criminal proceedings.
- It continued repressive wartime measures even after the First World War had ended.
- It was enacted despite strong Indian opposition.

The legislation was popularly condemned as the **Black Act**.

### 14.3 Opposition to the Rowlatt Act

Prominent Indian leaders who opposed the legislation included:

| Leader | Association |
|---|---|
| **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** | Opposed the legislation and resigned from the Imperial Legislative Council in protest |
| Madan Mohan Malaviya | Strongly opposed the legislation in the Imperial Legislative Council |
| **Mazharul Haque** | Opposed the legislation and resigned from the Imperial Legislative Council |
| Mahatma Gandhi | Organised a nationwide Satyagraha against the Act |

**Important distinction:** Jinnah and Mazharul Haque resigned in protest, but it would be incorrect to state that every elected Indian member of the Imperial Legislative Council resigned.

### 14.4 Historical Significance

- The Rowlatt Act intensified Indian opposition to British rule.
- Gandhi established the **Satyagraha Sabha in Bombay in 1919** to organise resistance.
- A nationwide hartal was observed on **6 April 1919**.
- The agitation marked Gandhi's first major attempt to organise an **all-India Satyagraha**.
- The protests and subsequent British repression contributed to the political unrest in Punjab, culminating in the **Jallianwala Bagh massacre on 13 April 1919**.

### 14.5 Important UPSC Prelims Facts

- **Defence of India Act:** 1915; provided extraordinary wartime powers.
- **Rowlatt Committee:** Sedition Committee, 1918.
- **Chairman:** Justice Sidney Rowlatt.
- **Rowlatt Act:** Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919.
- **Popular description:** Black Act.
- **Major provisions:** Preventive detention, special tribunals and restrictions on ordinary judicial safeguards.
- **Jinnah:** Resigned from the Imperial Legislative Council in protest.
- **Satyagraha Sabha:** Established by Gandhi in Bombay.
- **Nationwide hartal:** 6 April 1919.
- **Jallianwala Bagh massacre:** 13 April 1919.

## 15. Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919)

### 15.1 Background

- The **Rowlatt Act, 1919** authorised extraordinary measures against persons suspected of revolutionary activities, including preventive detention and special judicial procedures.
- The Act was passed despite strong opposition from Indian political leaders.
- Gandhi considered the legislation an attack on civil liberties and decided to organise a nationwide non-violent movement against it.

### 15.2 Organisation and Programme

| Feature | Fact |
|---|---|
| Year | 1919 |
| Leader | Mahatma Gandhi |
| Organisation | Satyagraha Sabha |
| Place of establishment | Bombay |
| Nationwide hartal | 6 April 1919 |
| Main objective | Non-violent opposition to the Rowlatt Act |
| Significance | Gandhi's first major attempt at organising an all-India Satyagraha |

**Methods of protest:**

- **Hartals:** Suspension of business and other normal activities as a form of protest.
- **Fasting and prayer:** Observed as expressions of self-discipline and peaceful resistance.
- **Public demonstrations:** Meetings and processions against the legislation.
- **Civil disobedience:** Peaceful violation of selected laws as part of the Satyagraha campaign.
- **Acceptance of punishment:** Participants were expected to remain non-violent and accept arrest or imprisonment.

### 15.3 Spread of the Movement

- The nationwide hartal on **6 April 1919** received considerable support in several cities.
- The agitation involved students, traders, workers and other sections of the population.
- It marked an important expansion of nationalist politics beyond the educated middle classes.
- Protests were particularly intense in Punjab, where political unrest and government repression escalated.

### 15.4 Suspension of the Satyagraha

- Violence occurred during demonstrations in several places, including Ahmedabad and parts of Punjab.
- Gandhi believed that the participants had not developed sufficient discipline to conduct a completely non-violent mass movement.
- He **suspended the Rowlatt Satyagraha in April 1919**.

**Significance:** The movement established Gandhi as a prominent all-India nationalist leader but also demonstrated the difficulties of maintaining non-violence during mass mobilisation.

**Prelims facts:**

- Rowlatt Act: **1919**.
- Satyagraha Sabha: **Bombay**.
- Nationwide hartal: **6 April 1919**.
- Suspension of Rowlatt Satyagraha: **April 1919**.

## 16. Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (13 April 1919)

### 16.1 Background

- Punjab witnessed widespread opposition to the Rowlatt Act.
- Two prominent nationalist leaders in Amritsar, **Dr Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr Satyapal**, were arrested and removed from the city on **10 April 1919**.
- Their arrest triggered demonstrations in Amritsar.
- Police firing on protesters and subsequent violence intensified the situation.
- British authorities imposed severe restrictions on public gatherings.

### 16.2 The Massacre

| Feature | Fact |
|---|---|
| Date | 13 April 1919 |
| Place | Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar, Punjab |
| Occasion | Baisakhi |
| Officer who ordered firing | Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer |
| Lieutenant-Governor of Punjab | Michael O'Dwyer |
| Official British death toll | 379 |

**Sequence of events:**

1. A large gathering assembled at Jallianwala Bagh on **13 April 1919**, the day of Baisakhi.
2. The gathering included people protesting against government repression and others present for the festival.
3. Brigadier-General **Reginald Dyer** arrived with armed troops.
4. Without issuing an adequate warning or allowing the crowd to disperse, Dyer ordered the troops to open fire.
5. The firing continued for several minutes, causing numerous deaths and injuries.

**Casualties:**

- The official British inquiry recorded **379 deaths**.
- Indian estimates placed the number of deaths considerably higher.
- The massacre became a major turning point in the Indian national movement.

### 16.3 Reginald Dyer and Michael O'Dwyer

| Reginald Dyer | Michael O'Dwyer |
|---|---|
| Brigadier-General in the British Indian Army | Lieutenant-Governor of Punjab |
| Personally ordered firing at Jallianwala Bagh | Headed the provincial administration during the repression |
| His conduct was investigated by the Hunter Committee | Was assassinated by Udham Singh in 1940 |

**Prelims distinction:** Reginald Dyer and Michael O'Dwyer were different individuals with different roles.

### 16.4 Consequences

- The massacre caused widespread outrage throughout India.
- It seriously undermined Indian confidence in British rule.
- **Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood in 1919** as a protest against British repression.
- Gandhi returned the **Kaiser-i-Hind medal in August 1920**, during the development of the Non-Cooperation Movement.
- The incident contributed to the growing demand for a nationwide movement against British rule.

**Important distinction:** Gandhi did not return the Kaiser-i-Hind medal immediately after the massacre; he surrendered it in **1920**.

## 17. Hunter Committee (1919)

### 17.1 Establishment

The British government appointed an official committee to investigate the disturbances in Punjab and other areas during 1919.

| Feature | Fact |
|---|---|
| Official name | Disorders Inquiry Committee |
| Popular name | Hunter Committee |
| Appointed | 14 October 1919 |
| Chairman | Lord William Hunter |
| Main purpose | Investigation of the 1919 disturbances and official actions |
| Report | 1920 |

### 17.2 Composition

The committee included British and Indian members.

**Important Indian members:**

1. Sir Chimanlal Harilal Setalvad
2. Pandit Jagat Narayan
3. Sardar Sahibzada Sultan Ahmad Khan

### 17.3 Findings and Consequences

- The committee investigated the Jallianwala Bagh massacre and other incidents of repression.
- It criticised Reginald Dyer's decision to open fire and the excessive duration of firing.
- The British majority and Indian minority differed in the severity of their criticism.
- The Indian members submitted a **minority report** that was more critical of official actions.
- Dyer was relieved of his command and was not subsequently employed in India.
- He was **not criminally prosecuted** for the massacre.

### 17.4 Congress Inquiry Committee

The Indian National Congress conducted a separate inquiry into the Punjab disturbances.

**Important members included:**

- Mahatma Gandhi
- Motilal Nehru
- C.R. Das
- Abbas Tyabji
- M.R. Jayakar

The Congress inquiry strongly criticised the repression and documented the suffering of the affected population.

### 17.5 Hunter Committee vs Congress Inquiry

| Feature | Hunter Committee | Congress Inquiry Committee |
|---|---|---|
| Appointed by | British government | Indian National Congress |
| Nature | Official government inquiry | Independent nationalist inquiry |
| Chairman | Lord William Hunter | Motilal Nehru |
| Main subject | Disturbances of 1919 and official responses | Punjab repression and its consequences |
| Findings | Criticised Dyer; Indian minority expressed stronger criticism | Strongly condemned official repression |

**Prelims distinction:** The Hunter Committee investigating the Punjab disturbances of 1919 was different from the **Hunter Commission on Education (1882)**, which was chaired by Sir William Wilson Hunter.

## 18. Udham Singh and Michael O'Dwyer

### 18.1 Background

- Udham Singh was an Indian revolutionary associated with the struggle against British colonial rule.
- He sought to avenge the Jallianwala Bagh massacre.
- Michael O'Dwyer, the former Lieutenant-Governor of Punjab, had supported the repressive policies followed during the Punjab disturbances.

### 18.2 Assassination of Michael O'Dwyer

| Feature | Fact |
|---|---|
| Revolutionary | Udham Singh |
| Person assassinated | Michael O'Dwyer |
| Date | 13 March 1940 |
| Place | Caxton Hall, London |
| Alternative name used | Ram Mohammad Singh Azad |
| Execution | 31 July 1940 |

- Udham Singh shot Michael O'Dwyer at a public meeting in London.
- He was arrested, tried and sentenced to death.
- He was executed at Pentonville Prison, London.

**Prelims fact:** Udham Singh assassinated **Michael O'Dwyer**, not Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer.

## 19. Connection with the Gurdwara Reform Movement

### 19.1 Background

- The Jallianwala Bagh massacre also contributed to discontent within the Sikh community.
- The conduct of certain Sikh religious authorities, including the reported honouring of Reginald Dyer by the manager of the Golden Temple, caused resentment.
- These developments added to existing demands for reform of gurdwara administration.

### 19.2 Gurdwara Reform Movement

| Feature | Fact |
|---|---|
| Popular name | Akali Movement |
| Main period | Early 1920s |
| Region | Punjab |
| Main objective | Transfer of gurdwara management from hereditary mahants and other existing authorities to representative Sikh bodies |
| Important organisation | Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee (SGPC) |
| SGPC established | 1920 |
| Important legislation | Sikh Gurdwaras Act, 1925 |

### 19.3 Significance

- The movement sought reform of religious institutions and community control over Sikh shrines.
- It became an important component of political mobilisation in Punjab during the early 1920s.
- The **Sikh Gurdwaras Act, 1925** provided a legal framework for the management of specified Sikh gurdwaras.

**Prelims distinction:** The Akali or Gurdwara Reform Movement was primarily concerned with the **management and control of Sikh religious institutions**, although it also had important connections with the wider nationalist movement.
