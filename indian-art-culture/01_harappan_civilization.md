# Art and Culture of the Harappan Civilisation
### UPSC Civil Services Preliminary Examination – Study Notes

**Scope:** Town planning, architecture, sculpture, seals, pottery, terracotta objects, ornaments, crafts, religious practices, burial customs and important archaeological discoveries.

These notes focus on **factual information, site–artefact associations, materials, distinctive artistic features and common MCQ traps**. Pictures are included to help identify important Harappan artefacts and architectural structures.

---

## 1. Harappan Civilisation: Basic Facts

The Harappan Civilisation, also called the **Indus Valley Civilisation**, was a Bronze Age civilisation that flourished in northwestern parts of the Indian subcontinent.

| Feature | Important facts |
|---|---|
| Early Harappan phase | c. 3300–2600 BCE |
| Mature Harappan phase | c. 2600–1900 BCE |
| Late Harappan phase | c. 1900–1300 BCE (approximately) |
| Main cultural character | Urban Bronze Age civilisation |
| First excavated major site | Harappa (1921) |
| Excavator of Harappa | Daya Ram Sahni |
| Excavator of Mohenjo-daro | R. D. Banerji (1922) |
| Discovery announced | John Marshall (1924) |
| Script | Undeciphered |
| Important metals | Copper, bronze, gold, silver and lead |
| Iron | No established use in Mature Harappan culture |
| Important features | Town planning, drainage, standardised bricks, seals and specialised crafts |

**Important distinctions:**
- Harappan culture extended beyond the Indus River basin into Gujarat, Rajasthan, Haryana and other regions.
- It was a **Bronze Age**, not an Iron Age, civilisation.
- Its script remains undeciphered; therefore, interpretations of its religion and political organisation are largely archaeological.

## 2. Town Planning and Architecture

### 2.1 General characteristics

Harappan cities are especially known for their sophisticated urban planning.

**Important features:**

1. Streets frequently followed a grid-like pattern, intersecting approximately at right angles.
2. Many cities contained elevated, fortified sectors and lower residential areas.
3. Burnt bricks were extensively used at major urban centres.
4. Bricks were often manufactured in a standard proportion of **1 : 2 : 4** (thickness : width : length).
5. Houses commonly contained courtyards, bathing areas and sometimes private wells.
6. Covered drains were constructed along streets.
7. Household wastewater was discharged through channels into larger drains.
8. Inspection openings and arrangements for cleaning drains were provided.

**Prelims trap:** Not every Harappan settlement followed an identical layout. The division into a citadel and lower town was common but not universal.

### 2.2 Important architectural structures

| Structure | Site | Important features |
|---|---|---|
| Great Bath | Mohenjo-daro | Brick-lined tank with waterproofing |
| Great Granary (traditional identification) | Harappa | Large brick foundations; function debated |
| Great Hall / Pillared Hall | Mohenjo-daro | Large building with rows of pillars |
| Reservoirs and water-management system | Dholavira | Elaborate water conservation structures |
| Dockyard (conventional identification) | Lothal | Large brick-lined basin; function debated |
| Fire altars | Kalibangan | Brick installations interpreted as ritual structures |

**Important facts:**
- The figure depicts a young female standing in a confident posture.
- Her left arm is covered with numerous bangles.
- Her right hand rests on her hip.
- The figure demonstrates the Harappans' knowledge of **metal casting**.

#### Lost-wax technique (Cire Perdue)

1. A model of the desired object is prepared using wax.
2. The wax model is covered with clay.
3. Heating melts and removes the wax.
4. Molten metal is poured into the cavity.
5. After cooling, the clay mould is broken to obtain the metal object.

**Prelims trap:** The Dancing Girl is made of **bronze**, not terracotta or stone.

## 5. Harappan Seals

Seals are among the most distinctive artistic products of the Harappan Civilisation.

### 5.1 General characteristics

**Important facts:**
- Most characteristic Harappan stamp seals were made of **steatite**.
- Other materials included terracotta, copper and faience.
- Seals were generally square or rectangular.
- Many contain animal figures and short inscriptions.
- The inscriptions are written in the **undeciphered Harappan script**.
- Some seals have a perforated boss on the reverse, possibly for suspension.

**Possible functions:**
- Identification of individuals or groups.
- Marking ownership or control of goods.
- Commercial and administrative activities.
- Religious or symbolic purposes.

The precise functions of individual seals cannot always be established.

### 5.2 Unicorn Seal

**Important features:**
- Depicts a mythical one-horned animal.
- Frequently accompanied by an object conventionally called a standard or ritual stand.
- Often contains Harappan script above the animal.
- The unicorn is one of the most frequently represented motifs on Harappan seals.

**Prelims trap:** The Harappan unicorn is a conventional artistic motif; its depiction does not establish the existence of a real one-horned animal.

### 5.3 Pashupati Seal – Mohenjo-daro

**Important features:**
- **Site:** Mohenjo-daro.
- **Material:** Steatite.
- Depicts a central seated figure wearing a horned headdress.

### 6.1 Characteristics

- Most Harappan pottery was **wheel-made**.
- Plain pottery was more common than painted pottery.
- The characteristic decorated pottery was **black-on-red ware**.
- Pottery was generally well-fired.
- Decorative motifs included geometric patterns, plants, birds and animals.
- Polychrome pottery, involving multiple colours, was relatively rare.

### 6.2 Important decorative motifs

| Motif | Description |
|---|---|
| Intersecting circles | Overlapping circular designs |
| Pipal leaves | Stylised leaves of the pipal tree |
| Fish scales | Repeated curved patterns |
| Peacocks | Bird representations |
| Geometric designs | Triangles, lines, circles and chequered patterns |
| Floral designs | Flowers and other plant forms |

### 6.3 Important pottery types

**Plain pottery**
- Usually made of red clay.
- Commonly used for household purposes.

**Painted pottery**
- Black designs painted on a red surface.
- Geometric and naturalistic motifs.

**Perforated pottery**
- Vessels containing numerous holes.
- Their functions remain uncertain; some may have been used for straining liquids.

**Polychrome pottery**
- Decorated using multiple colours.
- Comparatively rare.

**Prelims distinction:** Harappan black-on-red pottery should not be confused with the **Painted Grey Ware (PGW)** and **Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW)** traditions associated primarily with later periods.

## 8. Harappan Faience

**Faience** was an artificially manufactured, glazed material used for ornaments and decorative objects.

Important features:
- Usually made from finely powdered quartz or silica-rich material.
- Covered with a glazed surface.
- Commonly found in blue-green shades.
- Used to manufacture beads, bangles, pendants and small objects.

**Prelims trap:** Faience is not a naturally occurring precious stone.

## 9. Harappan Religious Practices

The religious beliefs of the Harappans are difficult to reconstruct because their script remains undeciphered.

### 9.1 Important archaeological evidence

| Archaeological evidence | Possible interpretation |
|---|---|
| Female terracotta figurines | Fertility-related beliefs or other functions |
| Pashupati seal | Possible religious symbolism |
| Pipal tree representations | Possible sacred-tree symbolism |
| Animal motifs | Symbolic or religious significance |
| Fire installations | Possible ritual activities |
| Great Bath | Possible ritual bathing |

### 9.2 Tree and animal symbolism

- The **pipal tree** appears in Harappan artistic representations.
- Bulls, buffaloes, elephants, tigers and rhinoceroses appear on seals.
- Some seals contain composite or mythical animals.

**Important caution:** The presence of animal or tree motifs does not automatically establish their worship.

## 10. Harappan Burial Practices

Harappan burial practices provide information about funerary customs and material culture.

### 10.1 Important characteristics

- Extended inhumation burials were practised at several sites.
- Some graves contained pottery, ornaments and other objects.
- Burial customs varied across sites and periods.
- Evidence of cremation is limited and its interpretation is uncertain.
- Elaborate royal tombs comparable to those of ancient Egypt have not been established.

### 10.2 Important burial sites

| Site | Important discovery |
|---|---|
| Harappa | Cemetery R-37 and Cemetery H |
| Lothal | A burial containing two individuals, sometimes described as a double burial |
| Kalibangan | Different burial types, including symbolic burials |
| Rakhigarhi | Harappan burials, including skeletal remains |
| Sanauli | Copper-covered wooden coffins and vehicle remains belonging to a different, broadly contemporary cultural context |

**Important distinction:** Cemetery H at Harappa is associated with the **Late Harappan phase**, while Cemetery R-37 is associated with the Mature Harappan period.

The Sanauli discoveries should not automatically be classified as typical Mature Harappan burial practices.

**Important characteristics:**
- The script remains **undeciphered**.
- Most surviving inscriptions are short.
- It appears on seals, pottery, tablets and other objects.
- Many inscriptions appear to have been written from **right to left**.
- The language represented by the script has not been conclusively identified.
- There is no generally accepted bilingual inscription comparable to the Rosetta Stone.

**Prelims traps:**
- The Harappan script has not been conclusively identified as Sanskrit, Tamil or any other language.
- It is not established that Harappan writing was alphabetic.
- Its undeciphered status limits conclusions about Harappan religion, government and society.
